# Time-restricted eating and fasting

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for weight and metabolic improvements comparable to ordinary calorie reduction, **Preliminary** for weight-independent circadian benefits, multi-day fasting and human cellular recycling.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(daily eating cut-offs and periodic fasting practised independently across Buddhist, Christian, Islamic, Jewish and Hindu traditions for over a millennium)*.

> **In short:** confining daily eating to an eight-to-ten-hour window or fasting on set days lowers weight, blood sugar and blood pressure mainly because most people end up eating fewer calories.[^2] A 2026 Cochrane review of 22 randomised trials found intermittent fasting produces no more weight loss and no better quality of life than standard dietary advice.[^1] Finishing meals earlier in the day outperforms late-evening eating in pooled trials,[^3] yet a tightly controlled 2025 crossover trial found neither early nor late windows improved insulin sensitivity or lipids when calories stayed constant.[^7] Fasting is a practical meal-structure tool if it suits your day — not a metabolic shortcut, not proven to trigger human anti-ageing cell recycling on a 16-hour schedule,[^9] and unsafe for anyone with an eating-disorder history or blood-sugar medication.[^11]

---

## What it is

Changing *when* you eat rather than prescribing specific foods, using one of two patterns:

- **Time-restricted eating** — placing all daily calories within a consistent window (typically **6 to 10 hours**) and taking only water or non-caloric drinks for the remaining 14 to 18 hours.[^3]
- **Intermittent and periodic fasting** — alternating regular eating days with low-calorie or water-only days (such as alternate-day fasting or eating normally five days a week and cutting calories on two days).[^2]

Both create longer stretches without incoming energy, though in everyday practice their effects overlap heavily with eating less overall.[^4]

---

## What it does for you

### It matches standard calorie reduction for weight and metabolic markers

A 2025 *BMJ* network meta-analysis pooling **99 randomised clinical trials (6,582 adults)** compared time-restricted eating, whole-day fasting (such as 5:2), alternate-day fasting and continuous calorie restriction.[^2] Every strategy reduced body weight compared with unrestricted eating.[^2] Compared with ordinary daily calorie restriction, **time-restricted eating and whole-day fasting performed identically**, while alternate-day fasting showed a small extra edge (**−1.29 kg**, 95% CI [−1.99, −0.59]) mostly in shorter trials under 24 weeks — below the authors' 2 kg threshold for clinical importance.[^2]

A 2026 Cochrane systematic review of **22 randomised trials (1,995 adults with overweight or obesity)** reached an even sharper conclusion: intermittent fasting produced **little to no difference in weight loss or quality of life** compared with standard dietary advice, and only small, clinically uncertain weight drops compared with doing nothing.[^1]

### Earlier eating windows outperform late-evening windows

A 2026 network meta-analysis of **41 randomised trials (2,287 participants)** lasting more than one month found that time-restricted eating improved body weight, fat mass, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, fasting insulin and triglycerides compared with usual diets.[^3]

Timing made a clear difference. **Early time-restricted eating** (finishing the last meal before 17:00) reduced body weight by **1.15 kg more** (95% CI [−1.86, −0.45]) and fasting insulin by **3.32 μIU/ml more** (95% CI [−5.36, −1.28]) than **late time-restricted eating** (eating past 19:00), with high certainty of evidence.[^3] By contrast, shrinking the daily eating window from over 8 hours down to 8 hours or less showed inconsistent differences.[^3]

### Caloric equality removes most — though not all — of the gap

In a 12-month trial in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, **139 patients with obesity** were randomised to identical daily calorie targets (1,200–1,500 kcal for women, 1,500–1,800 kcal for men) either within an 8:00–16:00 eating window or without time limits.[^4] Weight loss did not differ significantly (**−8.0 kg vs −6.3 kg**, net difference −1.8 kg, 95% CI [−4.0, 0.4], *p* = 0.11), nor did waist circumference, body fat, blood pressure or blood lipids.[^4]

When trials *do* match calories tightly, results split. A 2026 meta-analysis separating isocaloric trials reported a modest residual edge for time-restricted eating on weight (**−1.46 kg**, 95% CI [−2.65, −0.26]) and fat mass (**−1.50 kg**).[^8] Similarly, a small supervised crossover study in **8 men with prediabetes** eating identical calories over six hours before 15:00 found improved insulin sensitivity, beta-cell responsiveness and blood pressure without weight loss — though fasting triglycerides rose.[^6] Yet a larger 2025 randomised crossover trial (**31 women with overweight or obesity**) comparing two weeks of early (08:00–16:00) and late (13:00–21:00) eight-hour eating at near-constant calories found **no improvement in insulin sensitivity, 24-hour glucose, blood lipids or inflammation** in either window, even though the schedule shifted participants' internal circadian clocks and sleep timing.[^7]

---

## How much you need to do

- **An 8-to-10-hour daily eating window** ending at least a few hours before bed (for instance, 09:00–19:00 or 10:00–18:00) captures the structure tested in most successful metabolic trials.[^3]
- **No magic threshold exists at 16 hours.** Meta-analyses have not found consistent metabolic superiority for eating windows under 8 hours compared with 8-to-10-hour windows.[^3]
- **Keep protein and total nutrition adequate.** In a 12-week trial where participants skipped breakfast and ate between noon and 20:00 without dietary guidance, total weight loss did not beat regular meal timing (**−0.94 kg vs −0.68 kg**, *p* = .63), while **appendicular lean mass index fell more** in the fasting subgroup (**−0.16 kg/m²**, *p* = .005).[^5]

**So: if structured meal timing helps you stop late-night snacking, a consistent 10-hour daytime window ending by early evening is simpler to keep and carries fewer downsides than extreme fasting.**

## Where the evidence stops

**Most benefits disappear when calories are controlled.** In long-term calorie-matched trials[^4] and tightly controlled isocaloric crossover studies,[^7] timing alone often adds little or nothing once overall intake is equal. For most people, fasting works as a boundary that trims mindless evening eating, not as a metabolic bypass around total food intake.[^2]

**Cellular recycling claims outrun human data.** Popular commentary promises that a 16-hour fast switches on deep cellular clean-up (*autophagy*) and slows ageing. Almost all mechanism evidence comes from mice and cell cultures; in humans, blood-based measurements of autophagic flux are still exploratory and did not even differ between a 12-hour overnight fast and one hour after a high-protein meal.[^9] There is no proven hourly threshold at which fasting reliably boosts human autophagy or extends human lifespan.[^9]

**Long-term very short eating windows carry an unresolved observational warning.** In a 2025 analysis of **19,831 US adults** followed for a median of 8.1 years, self-reported eating windows under **8 hours** were associated with **higher cardiovascular mortality (HR = 2.35**, 95% CI [1.39, 3.98]) compared with 12-to-14-hour windows, while all-cause mortality associations were inconsistent.[^10] Observational dietary recalls are vulnerable to confounding — people eating inside narrow windows may have been skipping meals because of illness, shift work or financial stress — so this does not prove harm from intentional 16:8 eating, yet it rules out claiming that short eating windows are proven to extend life.[^10]

**Lean mass can drop without resistance training and protein.** Beyond the TREAT trial's lean-mass drop,[^5] meta-analysis of time-restricted eating shows modest reductions in fat-free mass alongside fat loss (**−0.86 kg** in non-isocaloric trials; **−0.41 kg** in isocaloric trials).[^8]

**Unknowns:** whether multi-day water fasts offer any durable clinical advantage over safer dietary changes; why early eating windows outperform late windows in pooled trials yet show null metabolic change in two-week isocaloric tests;[^7] and long-term outcomes beyond one to two years in healthy normal-weight adults.

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that fasting melts fat regardless of how much you eat inside the window, or that hitting 16 hours triggers verified human anti-ageing repair. The Cochrane review found intermittent fasting no better than standard dietary advice for weight,[^1] and human autophagy measurements have not established a reliable 16-hour repair switch.[^9]

## Who should be careful

In the 2026 Cochrane review, trials recorded no serious adverse events in supervised overweight-adult studies, though minor symptoms — hunger, headache, dizziness, nausea, irritability, cold intolerance, constipation, diarrhoea and poor concentration — appeared across trials.[^1] Several groups face genuine risks.

**History of eating disorders or disordered eating.** In a survey of **2,762 adolescents and young adults aged 16 to 30**, engaging in intermittent fasting was strongly associated with binge eating, purging, compulsive exercise and overall eating-disorder psychopathology across women, men and non-binary respondents.[^11] Anyone with current or past anorexia, bulimia, binge eating or rigid food preoccupation should avoid fasting windows.

**Diabetes medication and blood-sugar instability.** *As a clinical and practical judgement:* people taking insulin, sulfonylureas or other glucose-lowering drugs can experience dangerous hypoglycaemia when delaying or skipping meals without medical dose adjustments. Consult a prescribing clinician before changing meal timing.

**Pregnancy, breastfeeding, childhood and frailty.** *As a practical judgement:* growing children and adolescents, pregnant or breastfeeding women, underweight individuals and frail older adults at risk of muscle loss should not restrict eating windows or fast without clinical supervision.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Long before circadian biology, major traditions independently restricted eating hours or instituted periodic fasts — framing them around physical lightness, mental clarity and self-restraint rather than weight loss.

In early Buddhism, the *Bhaddāli Sutta* records the Buddha advising monks to take food in a single daytime session before noon and abstain from eating at night, explicitly citing freedom from illness and bodily sluggishness — while also accommodating a monk who found a strict single sitting destabilising.[^12] In early Christianity, the *Didache* instructed communities to fast twice weekly on Wednesdays and Fridays.[^12] And in Islam, the *Qur'an* prescribes fasting from dawn until sunset across the month of Ramadan while explicitly exempting those who are ill or travelling and stating that the rule is not meant to impose hardship.[^12]

You can test the practical value safely without extremes: finish dinner three hours before sleep and avoid late-night snacking for two weeks, and observe whether morning hunger, digestion and sleep settle or whether fasting leaves you irritable and preoccupied with food.
