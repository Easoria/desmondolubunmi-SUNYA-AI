# Mental imagery and visualisation

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for motor skill rehearsal, mental contrasting and clinical imagery rescripting, **Preliminary** for standalone positive imagery on depression.

**Traditional convergence — Strong** *(deliberate mental scene-building and visual concentration practised in Buddhist and Christian traditions for centuries)*.

> **In short:** mentally rehearsing a physical skill improves performance, especially when paired with actual practice,[^1] and pairing a realistic goal with its main obstacle and a concrete plan produces a small-to-moderate gain in following through.[^6] In clinical therapy, rewriting distressing memories in imagination matches established trauma and anxiety treatments.[^7] The catch sits in *what* people usually picture: pleasant fantasies about already having succeeded lower physical energy[^5] and predict worse real-world performance,[^4] and positive-imagery training on its own failed to beat a sham control in a large depression trial.[^9] Imagery is a useful rehearsal and therapeutic tool — not a substitute for action, and roughly four percent of people cannot form visual mental images at all.[^11]

---

## What it is

Deliberately generating sensory experiences in the mind — sights, movements, sounds, textures or felt effort — without an external stimulus present.[^10] In practice, it takes three distinct forms that do very different things:

- **Process and motor rehearsal** — picturing the physical sensations and step-by-step sequence of performing an action or preparing for a task.[^2]
- **Outcome fantasy versus mental contrasting** — picturing how good a future result would feel on its own, versus holding that wish alongside the main obstacle in your way and an if-then plan.[^6]
- **Guided and therapeutic imagery** — constructing a calming scene to settle arousal,[^8] or revisiting and changing a distressing autobiographical memory with a clinician.[^7]

Mental imagery acts as an emotional amplifier across all of these, evoking stronger emotional reactions than thinking about the same event in words.[^10]

---

## What it does for you

### Mental rehearsal improves physical and motor skills

Across sport psychology interventions, imagery training produced a medium overall improvement (**d = 0.431**) across performance, motivation and affective measures, and combining imagery with physical practice beat physical practice alone.[^1]

When a strict replication examined **37 studies** published between 1995 and 2018 and adjusted for publication bias, the standalone effect of mental practice on performance was **small but significant (r = 0.131)**.[^2] Gains depended on task type, session duration and imagery type — rehearsing the felt movement worked better than watching from the outside, and the benefit faded as the gap between mental practice and real execution widened.[^2]

### Rehearsing the *steps* works better than picturing the *prize*

In a classic experiment, **101 undergraduates** spent five minutes a day for five to seven days before a midterm exam picturing either the steps of studying well, getting a high grade, both, or logging hours as a control.[^3] Students who pictured the studying process studied roughly three hours more and scored higher (**80.60%** against **72.57%** in the outcome-only group), mediated by clearer planning and lower anxiety — though the overall grade difference across all four groups was marginal (*p* < .09).[^3] Students who pictured only the high grade studied the least of any group.[^3]

By contrast, pairing a desired outcome with the concrete obstacle standing in the way and an if-then plan (**mental contrasting with implementation intentions**) improved goal attainment across **21 field studies covering 15,907 participants** with a small-to-moderate pooled effect (**Hedges' g = 0.336**).[^6]

### Clinical imagery rescripting matches established treatments for distressing memories

In an updated meta-analysis of **17 randomised controlled trials (908 patients)** across post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety disorders, depression and eating disorders, therapist-guided imagery rescripting outperformed passive waitlist controls (**g = 0.68**, 95% CI [0.18, 1.18]).[^7] Compared head-to-head against established therapies — prolonged exposure, cognitive restructuring and EMDR — there was **no significant difference (g = −0.01**, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.15]), indicating comparable effectiveness across a short course of sessions.[^7]

### Guided imagery reduces distress in medical settings

Across **9 randomised trials (837 adult cancer patients)**, audio or clinician-guided imagery — alone or paired with progressive muscle relaxation — produced large reductions in anxiety (**SMD = −1.30**, 95% CI [−1.98, −0.63]) and depression (**SMD = −1.11**, 95% CI [−1.71, −0.51]), and improved quality of life (**SMD = 1.11**).[^8]

---

## How much you need to do

- **Five minutes a day for five to seven days** was enough for process rehearsal before an upcoming exam.[^3]
- **A few focused minutes** (often five to ten minutes in writing or mentally) is the typical dose for a single mental-contrasting and obstacle-planning exercise.[^6]
- **20 to 30 minutes per session** is the upper practical window identified in motor-practice reviews before cognitive fatigue sets in, timed close to when you will physically perform the skill.[^2]
- **Four to sixteen guided sessions** is typical for clinical imagery rescripting,[^7] while no standard session length or frequency is established for calming guided imagery.[^8]

**So: five minutes for process rehearsal or obstacle planning, and up to 20 minutes for skill rehearsal or calming imagery, matches what the trials tested.** Keep skill rehearsal close in time to actual physical practice.

## Where the evidence stops

**Positive outcome fantasies actively sap energy and effort.** Across four longitudinal studies tracking job seekers, students, romantic crushes and hip-replacement patients, positive *expectations* (judging success as likely based on experience) predicted higher effort and success, whereas pleasant *fantasies* about a desired future predicted *lower* effort and worse outcomes up to two years later.[^4] In four follow-up experiments, indulging positive fantasies lowered subjective feelings of energy and reduced systolic blood pressure compared with questioning or factual thought.[^5]

**Standalone positive imagery training did not beat a control for depression.** In a well-powered randomised trial of **150 adults with current major depression**, four weeks (12 sessions) of internet-delivered positive mental imagery training produced **no difference on the primary outcome** of depression symptom reduction compared with a sham control.[^9] People with depression often struggle to generate vivid positive images in the first place.[^9]

**Publication bias probably inflates behaviour-change averages.** The authors of the 21-study meta-analysis on mental contrasting explicitly warn that publication bias may make the true effect smaller than the published **g = 0.336** average.[^6]

**Roughly four percent of people do not form visual images.** General-population screening across two samples (**N = 1,004**) found that **3.9%** meet criteria for **aphantasia** (absent or markedly weak voluntary visual imagery), with **0.8%** reporting no visual imagery at all.[^11] Standard "picture yourself on a beach" instructions do not work for this group, who often use verbal, spatial or bodily cues instead.[^11]

**Unknowns:** whether calming guided imagery produces comparable reductions in anxiety among healthy adults outside hospitals, where few large trials exist;[^8] how long mental-contrasting gains last beyond initial follow-ups;[^6] and which alternative sensory cues work best for people with weak visual imagery.[^11]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that picturing an outcome you want makes it more likely to happen, or replaces planning and physical effort. Unanchored positive fantasies lower physiological readiness to act[^5] and predict poorer real-world achievement.[^4]

## Who should be careful

**Intrusive imagery, trauma and bipolar mood swings.** Because mental imagery amplifies emotion more strongly than words, deliberately dwelling on vivid scenes can intensify fear, shame or mania as easily as calm.[^10] In post-traumatic stress disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder or bipolar disorder, vivid mental images often drive flashbacks or escalating mood episodes.[^10]

**Clinical imagery rescripting is not a solo exercise.** The positive clinical results for aversive memories come from structured psychotherapy with a trained clinician, not self-directed revisiting of traumatic events.[^7] *As a practical judgement:* if closing your eyes and visualising triggers distressing flashbacks, panic or overwhelming agitation, stop, open your eyes and shift attention to concrete physical surroundings or seek clinical care.

**If you see nothing at all.** Straining to force mental pictures when you have weak visual imagery or aphantasia produces frustration for no gain.[^11] Switch to bodily rehearsal, written obstacle planning or non-visual practices instead.

## Traditional convergence

Strong. Two major contemplative traditions developed explicit, step-by-step instructions for using mental images deliberately — one to focus attention, the other to deepen ethical and emotional engagement.

In early Buddhist commentary, Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* describes staring steadily at a simple physical disc of clay or colour until a clear, stable mental counterpart image (*paṭibhāga-nimitta*) arises with the eyes closed, serving as a quiet anchor for sustained concentration.[^12] Around the same period in East Asia, the *Amitāyurdhyāna Sūtra* (*Contemplation Sūtra*) set out sixteen progressive visualisations — starting with holding the image of the setting sun steadily whether the eyes are open or closed — to train undivided attention.[^12] Independently, in 16th-century Christian practice, Ignatius of Loyola opened his *Spiritual Exercises* with "composition, seeing the place" (*compositio loci*), instructing practitioners to build a concrete mental scene of a road, room or hillside using sight and the other senses before beginning reflection.[^12]

You can test the practical distinction directly: spend five minutes imagining an upcoming difficult conversation or task step by step — including the moment you get stuck and how you respond — and compare how prepared you feel afterwards against spending five minutes picturing everything going effortlessly well.
