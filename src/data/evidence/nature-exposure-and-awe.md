# Nature exposure and awe

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for blood pressure, stress markers, mood and clinical anxiety/depression from nature exposure, **Preliminary** for standalone awe interventions and cognitive attention restoration.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(wilderness, forest and open-sky contemplation used independently across Daoist, Buddhist and Hebrew traditions to quiet agitation and put self-concern in proportion)*.

> **In short:** spending time outdoors in green or coastal spaces lowers physiological stress markers and blood pressure,[^1] cuts ruminative brooding,[^3] and shows meaningful reductions in anxiety and depression across clinical trials.[^2] Large population data point to roughly **120 minutes a week** as a threshold associated with better health and well-being.[^8] The awe piece is newer and thinner: weekly "awe walks" lifted daily joy and prosocial emotion in older adults without shifting anxiety or depression,[^6] and awe tied to fear or threat lowers well-being rather than helping it.[^11] Nature exposure does real work even when you feel nothing grand; attention-outward awe appears to be an added amplifier rather than a requirement.[^6]

---

## What it is

Two overlapping routes that are often sold together:

- **Nature exposure** — spending time in green spaces (parks, woods, gardens) or blue spaces (rivers, lakes, coast), sitting, resting or walking.
- **Awe** — the emotional response to encountering something vast that does not fit neatly into your usual frame of reference (a wide horizon, night sky, towering canopy, storm, or complex music or craft), which shifts attention outward and shrinks self-preoccupation.[^6]

You get physiological and restorative effects from ordinary local parks without waiting for a dramatic landscape.[^5] Experiencing awe adds a specific shift in perspective — away from inward self-monitoring — though nature and awe can happen without one another.[^6]

---

## What it does for you

### It lowers physiological stress markers and cardiovascular load

A meta-analysis pooling **143 studies (103 observational and 40 interventional)** found that higher greenspace exposure was associated with measurable drops in **salivary cortisol (−0.05)**, **heart rate (−2.57 bpm)** and **diastolic blood pressure (−1.97 mmHg)**, alongside higher **high-frequency heart rate variability** and lower long-term risk of type II diabetes and cardiovascular mortality.[^1]

In an eight-week daily-life study tracking salivary stress hormones directly, a **20-to-30-minute** walk or sit in a natural urban setting produced the steepest drop in **salivary cortisol (~21% per hour)** and **salivary amylase (~28% per hour)** after adjusting for normal diurnal decline.[^5]

### It reduces anxiety, depression and repetitive brooding

Pooling **14 clinical studies** of adults diagnosed with mild-to-moderate anxiety, depression or stress, nature-based health interventions reduced **anxiety (SMD = −0.80**, 95% CI [−1.56, −0.04]) and **depression (SMD = −0.87**, 95% CI [−1.18, −0.56]), and raised overall mental health scores (**SMD = 0.58**, 95% CI [0.39, 0.77]).[^2] Pooled stress reductions did not reach statistical significance (**SMD = −0.32**, 95% CI [−0.74, 0.09]).[^2]

On a single-walk timescale, healthy adults assigned to a **90-minute walk** in a natural setting showed reduced self-reported rumination and decreased neural activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (a region linked to repetitive self-focused brooding), whereas a 90-minute walk along a busy urban street produced neither change.[^3]

### It offers modest recovery for working memory and cognitive flexibility

Across **42 experimental studies (49 cognitive outcome measures)**, exposure to natural environments produced small-to-moderate improvements in **working memory (g = 0.162 across 22 outcomes)** and **cognitive flexibility**, with less reliable gains in attentional control.[^4] Real outdoor environments outperformed virtual nature scenes across those domains.[^4]

### Deliberately noticing awe lifts daily prosocial emotion — with clinical trials just beginning

In a randomised trial of **60 healthy older adults**, taking a weekly **15-minute outdoor "awe walk"** for eight weeks — prompted to notice vastness and detail around them — increased joy and prosocial positive emotions, lowered daily distress outside the walks, and produced noticeably smaller self-framing in participants' walk photographs compared with regular outdoor walks.[^6] However, baseline-to-post **anxiety, depression and life satisfaction scores did not change in either group**.[^6]

In a 2025 trial of **68 adults living with long COVID**, a four-week online training in finding brief moments of awe three times a day reduced depressive symptoms (**−17%**) and stress (**−12%**) and increased well-being (**+16%**) relative to a waitlist control.[^7]

---

## How much you need to do

- **20 to 30 minutes per visit** gave the most efficient drop in salivary cortisol in daily urban nature sessions.[^5]
- **120 minutes or more per week** was the threshold in a nationally representative English survey (**N = 19,806**) at which odds of reporting good health (**OR = 1.59**) and high well-being (**OR = 1.23**) rose reliably above zero-contact baselines — whether done in one long trip or several short local visits, peaking between 200 and 300 minutes a week.[^8]
- **15 minutes once a week** with attention turned outward to vastness and detail was enough to shift daily positive emotion over eight weeks.[^6]

**So: 20 to 30 minutes outdoors in a green or coastal spot, adding up to roughly two hours across the week, is the clearest target the data point to.** You do not need remote wilderness; most visits in the two-hour survey took place within two miles of home.[^8]

## Where the evidence stops

**The 120-minute weekly rule is observational, not a randomised trial.** Even after controlling for neighbourhood greenspace, income and physical activity, self-selected survey data (**N = 19,806**) cannot fully separate time in nature from sunlight exposure, movement or better underlying mobility.[^8]

**Clinical nature trials carry low-to-moderate certainty.** In the 2025 clinical meta-analysis of diagnosed anxiety and depression, wide variation in activities, control groups and risk of bias limited certainty under GRADE criteria, and overall perceived stress did not differ significantly from controls.[^2]

**Attention restoration effects are smaller and narrower than popular summaries claim.** An earlier systematic review of **31 attention-restoration studies** found significant improvements on only three measures (Digit Span Forward, Digit Span Backward and Trail Making Test B) while **ten other pooled cognitive tests showed no marked benefit**.[^9] A critical methodological review further argued that Attention Restoration Theory's core idea ("soft fascination") has never been given a clear, testable measurement definition.[^10]

**Clinical awe evidence is early and conflicted.** In the older-adult awe-walk trial, depression, anxiety and life satisfaction scores remained flat despite daily emotion gains.[^6] Meanwhile, the 2025 long-COVID awe trial relied on a waitlist control rather than an active comparison, used completers-only analysis, and included developers of the commercial awe programme as co-authors.[^7]

**Unknowns:** how much of nature's mood benefit comes from physical movement, daylight and stepping away from indoor work rather than vegetation itself;[^1] why some people get larger cognitive restoration than others;[^4] and whether brief awe prompts work reliably in active clinical depression without wider support.[^7]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that nature only counts if it is dramatic wilderness, or that if you fail to feel breathless wonder on a walk you missed the benefit. Ordinary urban parks lower cortisol in 20 minutes[^5] and meet the two-hour weekly threshold.[^8]

---

## Who should be careful

**Threat-based awe can worsen anxiety and fear.** Not all awe feels good. Studies distinguishing positive awe from **threat-based awe** (triggered by dangerous heights, violent storms, overwhelming scale or frightening events) show that threat-based awe increases fear, sympathetic arousal and powerlessness rather than improving well-being.[^11] If an environment makes you feel unsafe, cornered or alarmed, leave — fear cancels restoration.

*The practical cautions below are not derived from the clinical trials cited here, and are marked as such.*

**Physical environment and personal safety.** Allergies, seasonal pollen, asthma triggers, extreme heat or cold, ticks, uneven ground and unsafe parks are real risks. Choose familiar, accessible routes suited to your health and mobility.

**Eco-anxiety and grief.** For people distressed by visible environmental damage, heatwaves or habitat loss, certain outdoor trips can sharpen distress rather than soothe it; stick to restorative local spots when energy is low.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Long before environmental psychology, three independent traditions observed that wide natural scale quiets restless self-focus and puts human worries in proportion.

In Daoism, Chapter 17 of the *Zhuangzi* ("Autumn Floods") tells of the Spirit of the Yellow River, swollen with seasonal rain and pleased with his own importance until he flows east into the North Sea, sees no end to the water, and immediately recognises his own smallness.[^12] In early Buddhism, the *Cūḷasuññata Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 121) uses stepping out of village noise to steady the mind on the quiet "perception of forest" (*araññasaññā*) as the very first rung of mental quietness before deeper stillness.[^12] And in the Hebrew *Psalms*, Psalm 8 captures the exact cognitive shift later measured as "small self": looking up at the night sky, moon and stars prompts the immediate question of what one small human life amounts to against that scale.[^12]

You can test both halves on any ordinary walk: spend 20 minutes outdoors without headphones or phone, look up at the sky or into the canopy rather than down at the pavement, and notice whether your internal monologue loosens its grip.
