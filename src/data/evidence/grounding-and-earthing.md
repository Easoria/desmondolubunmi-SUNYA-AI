# Grounding and earthing

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Preliminary** *(several small pilot studies report shifts in sleep ratings, blood viscosity or muscle-recovery markers, yet no systematic meta-analysis or large independent randomised trial exists, mood and soreness results are mixed, and foundational papers carry commercial conflicts of interest)*.

**Traditional convergence — Weak** *(modern electrical earthing originated in the late 1990s; barefoot walking on grass appears in nineteenth-century European nature cure as cold stimulation, with no electrical theory)*.

> **In short:** "earthing" claims that touching bare soil or sleeping on conductive mats wired to an outlet's ground pin draws free electrons into the body to neutralise chronic inflammation.[^1] After more than twenty years, there is still no large-scale independent randomised trial or meta-analysis confirming those broad claims, and the proposed antioxidant-electron biochemistry conflicts with established physiology.[^2] A handful of small sham-controlled pilots report isolated gains in sleep quality[^7] or post-exercise blood markers,[^5] while failing to improve anxiety or depression[^6] or felt muscle soreness.[^5] Walking barefoot on grass feels pleasant mostly for obvious reasons — daylight, movement and time outdoors — not because your heels are absorbing electrons.

---

## What it is

Placing bare skin in direct contact with outdoor earth, grass, sand or water — or indoors using conductive mats, patches or sheets plugged into the earth port of a wall socket — with the stated aim of equalising the body's electrical potential with the Earth's surface.[^1]

This is completely separate from **psychological grounding** (sensory-orientation exercises used during panic or dissociation, such as naming five things you see or pressing your feet into the floor to bring attention back to the room). Psychological grounding works through attention, not electrical conductivity.

---

## What it does for you

### Electrical potential on the skin changes immediately — health outcomes remain unsettled

Touching bare ground or a grounded mat measurably drops alternating-current voltage induced on the skin by surrounding household wiring, including in a clinical study of **26 preterm infants** inside neonatal intensive care incubators where reducing incubator-induced voltage coincided with higher short-term vagal tone.[^8] Demonstrating that skin voltage drops to earth potential is straightforward physics; demonstrating that this change heals chronic adult disease is a separate claim that has not been established.[^2]

### Small pilots report isolated gains alongside clear nulls

The clinical evidence consists of small pilot trials (typically **10 to 60 participants**) rather than large confirmatory studies:[^2]

- **Sleep ratings:** In a 12-week double-blind pilot of **22 patients with mild Alzheimer's disease** (15 completing), 30 minutes of mat grounding five times weekly improved Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores compared with sham mats (*p* = 0.006), while **anxiety and depression scores did not change significantly**.[^6] A 31-day sham-controlled mat pilot in **60 adults** reported improved sleep ratings and actigraphy-estimated sleep duration, though it remains a single preliminary pilot awaiting independent replication.[^7]
- **Exercise recovery:** In a triple-blinded trial of **22 healthy adults** recovering from downhill treadmill running, grounded sleeping showed smaller drops in jump performance and strength and smaller rises in creatine kinase over 10 days, **but produced no significant reduction in felt muscle soreness**.[^5]
- **Pain and mood in bodyworkers:** A six-week double-blind stepped-wedge trial of **16 massage therapists** reported improved self-rated pain, physical function and fatigue during grounded weeks.[^9]

---

## How much you need to do

- **30 to 60 minutes daily** on a mat[^6] or **6 to 8 hours overnight**[^7] are the protocols tested in existing pilot studies.
- **No reliable dose-response or minimum effective time is established**, because independent replication across large samples has not been done.[^2]

**So: there is no evidence-backed earthing dose to prescribe.** If you enjoy walking barefoot safely outdoors on clean grass or sand, treat it as ordinary time outside rather than an electrical therapy.

## Where the evidence stops

**No large independent trial or meta-analysis exists.** After more than fifteen years of published claims, no randomised controlled trial of 100 or more participants has independently confirmed earthing's health effects, and the literature has not progressed beyond small preliminary pilots.[^2]

**The core biological mechanism is implausible.** Proponents claim free electrons entering through the soles of the feet travel to sites of inflammation to neutralise reactive oxygen species.[^1] In established biochemistry, reactive oxygen species are neutralised locally inside cells within fractions of a second by intracellular enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and glutathione — a chemical environment that free electrical charges entering via the skin cannot selectively navigate.[^2]

**Foundational studies had no controls and carry commercial conflicts.** The widely cited 2004 sleep-and-cortisol study tracked **12 people** with **no control group and no blinding**.[^3] The 2013 blood-viscosity study tested **10 healthy adults** sitting in a dimmed soundproof room for two hours with **no sham-mat control group**.[^4] Crucially, the authors of both that viscosity study[^4] and the main 2015 inflammation review[^1] explicitly disclose serving as **paid independent contractors and shareholders** in EarthFx Inc., the commercial entity funding earthing research.

**Time outdoors explains much of the ordinary benefit without electricity.** Walking barefoot on a lawn or beach combines daylight, gentle movement, temperature sensation and nature exposure — variables that measurably lower stress on their own while wearing shoes.

**Unknowns:** whether reducing ambient 60-Hz household voltage on the skin during sleep produces any reproducible benefit in genuinely independent trials;[^8] why mood failed to improve in the Alzheimer's mat pilot[^6] and why perceived soreness failed to improve in the exercise-recovery trial[^5] that reported sleep or enzyme shifts; and how much reported benefit in open-label pilots comes from placebo expectation.

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that wearing rubber-soled shoes leaves you electrically "starved" and drives chronic inflammation,[^1] or that a conductive mat wired to an outlet is an anti-inflammatory medical treatment.[^2]

## Who should be careful

**Diabetes, peripheral neuropathy and poor circulation.** People with diabetes or nerve damage in their feet can sustain cuts, burns on hot pavement or puncture injuries without feeling them, leading to slow-healing ulcers and severe infection; major diabetes clinical guidelines advise **against walking barefoot**, even indoors.[^10]

**Barefoot puncture wounds and soil pathogens.** Walking barefoot outdoors exposes feet to thorns, glass and splinters capable of causing deep soft-tissue or bone infections (osteomyelitis) from routine outdoor punctures.[^11] Check ground surfaces carefully and wear protective footwear wherever debris, thorns or hot surfaces are present.

*The electrical caution below is a practical judgement rather than a trial finding, and is marked as such.*

**Improperly wired household outlets.** Indoor grounding mats and sheets rely on your wall outlet's earth port being wired accurately. Using unverified outlets, faulty adapters or uncertified mats introduces unnecessary electrical hazard; never connect homemade wires to mains sockets, and disconnect conductive mats during electrical storms.

## Traditional convergence

Weak. The modern concept of electrical earthing — connecting the human body to Earth's surface electrons via bare skin or outlet-wired mats — was coined in the late 1990s.

While barefoot living was simply everyday necessity throughout most of human history, deliberate barefoot walking as a formal health regimen appears most clearly in nineteenth-century European nature cure: the Bavarian priest Sebastian Kneipp's 1886 book *Meine Wasserkur* prescribed walking barefoot on dew-wet grass, wet stones or shallow water to harden circulation and stimulate the nervous system through cold contact.[^12] That tradition treated cold and tactile stimulation as the active ingredient, with no concept of electrical charge transfer.

You can separate the two directly in your own experience: spend twenty minutes walking slowly in a quiet park or garden with comfortable shoes on, and compare how calm or rested you feel against twenty minutes barefoot on clean grass — noticing whether any shift comes from unhurried outdoor attention rather than bare soles.
