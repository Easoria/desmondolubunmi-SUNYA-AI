# Diet composition

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Strong** for cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes, **Moderate** for depressive symptoms.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(systematic distinctions between nourishing, agitating and dulling foods appear independently across Ayurvedic, Chinese medical, Greek and yogic traditions for over two thousand years)*.

> **In short:** eating mostly minimally processed, fibre-rich whole foods — in the pattern of a Mediterranean diet — lowers the risk of death and major cardiovascular events in randomised trials,[^1] and higher daily fibre intake tracks with 15% to 30% lower mortality across large cohorts.[^2] When it comes to weight loss, low-carbohydrate, low-fat and balanced diets produce nearly identical results by twelve months once adherence is equal.[^3] On mood, improving overall diet produces a small-to-moderate drop in depressive symptoms in trials,[^6] whereas handing people multi-nutrient pills did not prevent depression over one year.[^8]

---

## What it is

What your daily meals are actually made of — the balance of minimally processed whole foods versus ultra-processed packaged products, dietary fibre, fats, protein and carbohydrates — rather than *when* you eat them.

Two findings define the current evidence: overall **dietary pattern** (high vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and olive or plant oils, low ultra-processed foods) matters consistently,[^1] while ultra-processed meals drive spontaneous overeating even when nutrients are matched on paper.[^4] By contrast, **macro ratios** (low-carb versus low-fat) matter far less than marketed,[^3] and **isolated supplement pills** did not prevent depression in a large prevention trial.[^8]

---

## What it does for you

### A whole-food Mediterranean pattern reduces mortality and heart events

A 2023 *BMJ* network meta-analysis pooling **40 randomised controlled trials (35,548 patients at cardiovascular risk)** compared seven named dietary programmes against minimal advice.[^1] **Mediterranean diet programmes** showed moderate-certainty reductions across major clinical endpoints:[^1]

| Outcome | Odds ratio (vs minimal intervention) |
|---|---|
| All-cause mortality | **0.72** (95% CI [0.56, 0.92]) |
| Cardiovascular mortality | **0.55** (95% CI [0.39, 0.78]) |
| Non-fatal heart attack | **0.48** (95% CI [0.36, 0.65]) |
| Stroke | **0.65** (95% CI [0.46, 0.93]) |

Low-fat programmes also lowered all-cause mortality (**OR = 0.84**) and non-fatal heart attack (**OR = 0.77**), while five other popular named diets showed little or no benefit over minimal advice.[^1]

### Higher dietary fibre lowers metabolic risk across cohorts and trials

Commissioned by the World Health Organization, a *Lancet* synthesis pooled **185 prospective observational studies (~135 million person-years)** and **58 clinical trials (4,635 adults)**.[^2] Higher intake of dietary fibre was associated with a **15% to 30% lower risk** of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, coronary heart disease, stroke and type 2 diabetes in cohorts, with the largest risk reduction sitting between **25 g and 29 g of fibre per day**.[^2] In controlled trials, higher fibre intake lowered body weight, systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol.[^2]

### Cutting ultra-processed foods reduces spontaneous overeating

In a tightly controlled inpatient crossover trial, **20 weight-stable adults** lived in a metabolic ward for four weeks, eating ad libitum for 14 days on **ultra-processed** meals and 14 days on **unprocessed** meals matched on paper for presented calories, sugar, fat, sodium and fibre.[^4] On ultra-processed meals, participants ate faster and spontaneously consumed **508 calories per day more**, gaining **0.9 kg** over two weeks; on unprocessed meals, they lost **0.9 kg**.[^4]

### Whole-diet improvement lifts depressive symptoms — especially when already depressed

Across **16 randomised trials covering 45,826 mostly non-clinical adults**, whole-dietary improvement reduced depressive symptoms by a small margin (**g = 0.275**, 95% CI [0.10, 0.45]), while anxiety overall showed no significant drop (**g = 0.100**, *p* = 0.148).[^6]

When a 2025 meta-analysis focused on **5 randomised trials (1,507 adults with moderate-to-severe depression)**, Mediterranean-diet interventions reduced depressive symptom scores (**MD = −0.41**, 95% CI [−0.69, −0.13]) over 12 to 24 weeks — though few trials exist and participants cannot be blinded to what they eat.[^7]

## How much you need to do

- **25 to 29 grams of dietary fibre per day** from whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit and nuts is the range linked to the clearest risk reductions.[^2]
- **Shift meals toward minimally processed ingredients** cooked from whole foods rather than packaged ready meals, which drove passive calorie excess even when matched for nutrients on the label.[^4]
- **12 to 24 weeks** of consistent dietary support is the timeframe tested in clinical depression trials.[^7]
- **Pick a pattern you can stay on.** Across **121 randomised weight-loss trials (21,942 adults)**, low-carbohydrate, low-fat and moderate-macronutrient diets all produced roughly **4 to 5 kg** of weight loss at six months — and differences between named diets largely vanished by 12 months.[^3]

**So: build most meals around minimally processed whole plants, adequate protein and fibre around 25–29 g a day, in a pattern you can maintain past six months.**

## Where the evidence stops

**Named macronutrient diets tie in the long run.** Low-carb, low-fat and balanced diets produce virtually indistinguishable weight and blood-pressure outcomes by 12 months once adherence drops off, making diet wars mostly a distraction from total food quality and sustainability.[^3]

**Multi-nutrient supplement pills did not prevent depression.** In the MooDFOOD trial of **1,025 overweight adults with subsyndromal depressive symptoms** followed for one year, daily multi-nutrient supplements (omega-3s, selenium, folic acid, vitamin D and calcium) **failed to prevent major depressive episodes** compared with placebo — and performed slightly worse on symptom scores (*p* = .035).[^8] Isolated pills do not replicate whole meals.[^8]

**Ultra-processed food epidemiology still carries confounding.** While an umbrella review in *The BMJ* linked higher ultra-processed food exposure to 32 adverse health outcomes — with convincing evidence for cardiovascular death, anxiety/mental disorders and type 2 diabetes — most of that literature is observational, where low income, stress, smoking and poor sleep cluster alongside packaged food intake.[^5]

**Dietary mood trials cannot be double-blinded.** People know whether they were coached to cook Mediterranean meals or attend a social group, so expectation and clinician contact explain part of the drop in depression scores.[^7]

**Unknowns:** which aspects of ultra-processed food (texture and eating speed, calorie density, additives or missing food matrix) drive passive overeating most;[^4] whether dietary changes prevent major depression onset in clinical populations when weight does not change;[^8] and how strongly individual gut responses alter outcomes on identical foods.

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that one extreme elimination rule, magic superfood or daily supplement stack outperforms basic, affordable whole foods. Mediterranean programmes reduced major cardiovascular events in randomised trials,[^1] and named macronutrient diets converge once adherence fades.[^3]

## Who should be careful

*The cautions below are practical and clinical judgements rather than adverse-event statistics from the trials cited above, and are marked as such.*

**Eating disorders and rigid food preoccupation.** Treating food quality as moral purity or turning nutritional rules into anxiety-driven restriction can trigger or worsen orthorexia, anorexia or binge-restrict cycles. If thinking about food rules is raising distress or shrinking your social life, step back from tracking and consult a clinician.

**Allergies, coeliac disease and medical conditions.** General whole-food patterns (such as higher fibre, nuts, legumes or dairy) do not override diagnosed allergies, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel flares, kidney disease or medication interactions. Follow individualised medical nutrition advice where a health condition exists.

**Unnecessary elimination diets.** Dropping entire food groups without a medical reason risks micronutrient gaps, fatigue and social isolation around ordinary shared meals.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Long before macronutrient analysis, major medical and contemplative traditions independently categorised foods not just by hunger satisfaction, but by whether a meal left the body clear or sluggish.

In India, the *Bhagavad Gītā* divides food into three qualities: fresh, nourishing and naturally agreeable foods (*sāttvika*) that support vitality and steadiness; excessively spicy, sour, salty or burning foods (*rājasika*) that stir agitation; and stale, over-processed or impure foods (*tāmasika*) that produce dullness.[^9] Classical Ayurveda in the *Caraka Saṃhitā* treats wholesome food (*pathya*) taken in proper measure as the primary foundation of health and mental steadiness.[^9] In China, the *Huangdi Neijing Suwen* emphasises balancing whole grains, fruits, vegetables and moderate flavours so organs remain steady, ranking dietary adjustment ahead of harsh medicines.[^9] And in Greece, the Hippocratic treatise *On Ancient Medicine* anchors medicine itself in noticing how different foods and levels of refinement agree with healthy versus unwell digestion.[^9]

You can test the immediate signal in your own system without adopting any dogma: eat a simple, freshly cooked meal of whole ingredients for lunch on three workdays, and observe your afternoon energy, digestion and clarity compared with three days of packaged convenience food.
