# Sexual behaviour and abstinence

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for the association between partnered sex, physical affection and relationship well-being, **Preliminary** for sleep effects, prostate health and deliberate sexual or pornography abstinence.

**Traditional convergence — Strong** *(deliberate sexual restraint and temporary periods of abstinence documented across Indian yogic, Roman Stoic and early Christian traditions for nearly two thousand years)*.

> **In short:** in committed relationships, having sex rises with life satisfaction up to about **once a week**, after which more frequent sex adds no extra well-being gain[^1] — and much of that link runs through everyday physical affection rather than intercourse alone.[^2] Instructing couples to double how often they have sex actually lowers enjoyment.[^3] On the abstinence side, there is no solid evidence that holding back ejaculation produces lasting physiological boosts: the widely cited 2003 study claiming a seventh-day testosterone spike was retracted in 2021.[^7] Stepping back from compulsive pornography use can restore a sense of self-control when paired with sound habits,[^10] whereas shame-driven online abstinence forums are linked to heightened anxiety, erectile worries and post-slip distress.[^11]

---

## What it is

How you handle sexual expression and sexual restraint — across three distinct behaviours that research keeps separate even when internet culture mixes them together:

- **Partnered sexual activity and intimacy** — sexual contact alongside non-sexual physical affection (touch, holding, closeness) within a relationship.[^2]
- **Solo sexual activity** — masturbation with or without pornography.
- **Deliberate abstinence** — choosing to pause pornography, masturbation or all sexual activity for a set period or as an ongoing discipline.[^10]

The psychological and physiological effects of these three depend heavily on consent, relationship warmth and whether your behaviour conflicts with your own values.[^2] Moral incongruence between viewing and personal beliefs is a separate driver of distress from viewing volume alone.[^9]

---

## What it does for you

### Partnered sex tracks with higher well-being up to about once a week

Across three studies covering **30,645 adults**, sexual frequency was positively linked to happiness and life satisfaction **only for people in relationships**, and the relationship was curved rather than linear: well-being rose from less than monthly up to roughly **once a week**, then levelled off completely beyond that point.[^1] The well-being difference between having sex once a week versus less than once a month was larger than the well-being gap associated with a substantial jump in household income, and was explained by higher relationship satisfaction.[^1]

Four follow-up studies showed *why* much of that link holds: everyday **physical affection** (touching, kissing, hugging) strongly mediated the association between sexual activity and overall well-being.[^2]

### Sexual activity before bed may modestly reduce night-time waking

In a small 11-night home-EEG pilot study of **14 healthy cohabiting adults**, nights with either partnered sex or solo masturbation showed higher objectively measured **sleep efficiency** and less time awake after falling asleep than nights with no sexual activity.[^4] However, how quickly participants fell asleep and how well they rated their own sleep did not change, and solo masturbation pushed bedtimes noticeably later.[^4]

### Higher adult ejaculation frequency is linked to lower prostate cancer diagnoses

In an 18-year prospective follow-up of **31,925 men**, reporting **21 or more ejaculations per month** in adulthood was associated with roughly a **19% to 22% lower risk** of being diagnosed with prostate cancer compared with 4 to 7 ejaculations per month, after adjusting for lifestyle and PSA screening.[^5] The lower risk applied mainly to low- and intermediate-risk disease rather than aggressive metastatic prostate cancer.[^5]

### Stepping back from compulsive pornography use can restore a sense of control

A qualitative study of **104 abstinence journals** from men attempting to stop pornography use found that abstinence felt very hard at first, but became workable when combined with cognitive-behavioural strategies (changing bedtime routines, removing triggers, replacing boredom) and social support.[^10] Writers commonly reported regaining a sense of control over their habits and feeling clearer and more confident.[^10]

---

## How much you need to do

- **About once a week** is where the observational link between partnered sex and general well-being plateaus; pushing higher than that adds no average well-being benefit.[^1]
- **Keep frequency unforced.** In a three-month trial of **128 married adults**, couples randomly instructed to *double* their sexual frequency had more sex — and reported a **small drop in happiness and sexual enjoyment** because turning sex into a target removed intrinsic desire.[^3]
- **No fixed abstinence clock is validated.** Popular "30-day" or "90-day" abstinence timelines have no physiological or clinical trial basis establishing an optimal duration.[^10]

**So: treat partnered intimacy as an affectionate connection rather than a weekly quota, and if you pause pornography or solo habits to test what changes, run it as a calm 14-to-30-day behavioural check rather than an all-or-nothing streak.**

## Where the evidence stops

**The famous "seventh-day testosterone boost" study was retracted.** Online claims about "semen retention" lean heavily on a 2003 Chinese study reporting a peak in serum testosterone on day seven of abstinence — that paper was **formally retracted in 2021** for overlap with an earlier publication.[^7] The only other commonly cited experiment tested men after **three weeks** of sexual abstinence and found orgasm triggered the exact same blood-pressure, heart-rate, catecholamine and prolactin surge before and after abstinence; while testosterone was higher at the post-abstinence session (when anticipating an erotic film), abstinence without cues has not been established as raising long-term baseline testosterone.[^6]

**Masturbation abstinence is driven more by beliefs than by behaviour.** In a survey of **1,063 adults**, motivation to abstain from masturbation was driven mainly by believing masturbation is unhealthy, conservative attitudes, religiosity and lower trust in science — and showed **no significant correlation with actual orgasm frequency**.[^8]

**Feeling "addicted" to pornography often reflects moral conflict as much as viewing volume.** A meta-analytic synthesis showed that distress and self-perceived pornography addiction are strongly predicted by **moral incongruence** — experiencing a sharp clash between personal moral beliefs and one's own viewing — rather than hours watched alone.[^9]

**All sleep, prostate and journal findings are limited.** The home-EEG sleep trial tested just 14 young heterosexual adults in healthy relationships;[^4] the prostate cohort is observational and cannot prove ejaculation itself prevents cancer;[^5] and the 104 abstinence journals were self-selected, unverified self-reports with no comparison group.[^10]

**Unknowns:** whether stepping away from pornography without adding shame improves mood or focus in randomised controlled trials;[^10] how partnered non-sexual touch compares directly against sexual intercourse for daily stress recovery;[^2] and what effects temporary restraint has on attention outside monastic or religious contexts.

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that retaining semen builds measurable physiological "superpowers", raises resting testosterone long-term,[^7] or that having sex more than once a week keeps pushing happiness upward.[^1]

## Who should be careful

**Shame-heavy online abstinence forums.** In a preregistered survey of men participating in online "Reboot/NoFap" communities, heavier forum involvement was associated with **higher current depression, anxiety and erectile worries**, and many participants reported intense feelings of worthlessness or **suicidal thoughts** following a perceived "relapse" into masturbation or pornography — alongside frequent exposure to hostile or self-harm-encouraging forum posts.[^11] If an abstinence attempt makes you obsessive, self-hating or hopeless after a slip, leave those communities and drop streak counting.

**Erectile anxiety.** Worry about sexual performance — rather than masturbation or pornography frequency itself — strongly predicts erectile difficulties; framing normal arousal shifts as permanent brain damage worsens performance anxiety.[^11]

**Relationship conflict and coercion.** Never use either frequency targets or unilateral sexual abstinence as leverage, punishment or a test inside a relationship; turning sex into an obligation undermines desire and satisfaction.[^3]

*The two practical cautions below are clinical judgements rather than findings from the trials cited above, and are marked as such.*

**Compulsive sexual behaviour and trauma.** If sexual urges or pornography use feel genuinely out of control, cause serious harm to your work or relationships, or tie into trauma, seek qualified medical or psychological care rather than unguided online challenges.

**Safe sex and medical conditions.** Protected, consensual intimacy and existing cardiovascular or pelvic-pain advice from a doctor always take priority over general well-being guidelines.

## Traditional convergence

Strong. Three independent ancient traditions treated sexual restraint and temporary abstinence not as magic batteries, but as practical training against impulsiveness and distraction.

In classical Indian yoga, Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtras* lists sexual restraint (*brahmacarya*) among the five foundational ethical restraints (*yamas*) and states that when it is firmly established, steadiness and vigour (*vīrya*) follow — which classical commentary frames as restraint of the senses rather than mere fluid hoarding.[^12] In first-century Roman Stoicism, Musonius Rufus argued in his lecture *On Sexual Indulgence* that casual pleasure-seeking erodes self-command, restricting legitimate sexual expression to committed partnership.[^12] And in early Christianity, Paul's first letter to the Corinthians explicitly balanced both sides: recommending mutually agreed, *temporary* periods of abstinence inside marriage specifically to make room for focused prayer, while warning couples to resume intimacy afterwards rather than let prolonged deprivation turn into distress and loss of self-control.[^12]

You can test the practical core without adopting any creed: notice over two weeks how habitual, mindlessly scrolled sexual stimulation affects your mood and sleep compared with either genuine affectionate intimacy or a calm, shame-free pause.
