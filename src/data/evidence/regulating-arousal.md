# Regulating arousal

*A family of three mechanisms. Reviewed November 2026.*

**Research strength — Strong** for lowering blood pressure and for shifting the nervous system in either direction, **Moderate** for mood and anxiety.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** downward *(breath regulation independently across Indian, Chinese, Tibetan, Christian and Sufi practice)*, **Strong** upward *(bhastrikā, kapālabhāti and tummo)*.

> **In short:** the second mechanism behind the internal levers has nothing to do with thought. It changes how activated the body is — and it works in both directions. Slowing the breath to around six a minute lowers blood pressure by enough that a medical review called it a reasonable first option before medication,[^1] and shifts the nervous system measurably toward rest across one of the largest evidence bases in this framework.[^2] Going the other way, rapid breathing and breath holding rouse the system immediately, and ten days of training measurably altered the inflammatory response to a controlled immune challenge.[^3] Knowing which direction you need is the single most useful thing on this page.

---

## Why direction matters more than technique

Most people arrive at these practices wanting to be calmer, and reach for whichever one is nearest. But the body's level of activation is a dial with two ends, and the two ends suit different problems.

**Too activated** looks like a racing heart, shallow breath, tight chest, difficulty sitting still, waking at four in the morning, irritability that arrives before the thought that justifies it. This is the common case and it is what downregulation addresses.

**Too flat** looks like heaviness, grey mood, no drive to begin anything, sleeping long and waking unrefreshed. Downregulating someone already flat makes them flatter. What helps is the other direction.

The reason this matters clinically: **anxiety is substantially an arousal problem, not a thinking problem.** One of the most common reasons practice disappoints is handing someone a thought tool for a body problem — which is part of why meditation's results for anxiety are moderate while slow breathing has solid physiological evidence behind it. If your difficulty is a mind that will not stop circling, this is the wrong family; see [Quieting repetitive thought](/evidence/quieting-repetitive-thought).

## The two directions

### Downward — lowering activation

| Mechanism | What the research shows | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow and paced breathing** | **Strong.** Blood pressure down 5.6/3.0 mmHg across 17 trials; parasympathetic shift across 223 studies; stress, anxiety and low mood improved by a small-to-moderate margin, and the benefit grew with days practised | [Slow and paced breathing](/evidence/slow-and-paced-breathing) |
| **Progressive muscle relaxation and stillness** | **Moderate.** Reduced anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms across 46 publications; subjective sleep quality improved across 31 trials; systolic pressure down 7.5 mmHg short-term, though certainty was graded very low | [Progressive muscle relaxation and stillness](/evidence/progressive-muscle-relaxation-and-stillness) |

Two cautions sit on these pages rather than here, and both are counterintuitive enough to flag: **trying to relax harder in bed can worsen chronic insomnia**, and a real subgroup of chronically anxious people experience a *paradoxical rise* in anxiety when releasing muscular tension.

### Upward — raising activation

| Mechanism | What the research shows | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Activating breathwork** | **Strong** for acute activation — carbon dioxide falls measurably within seconds, with a sharp adrenaline rise — and for modulating the inflammatory response: ten days of training produced a 53% reduction in TNF-α and a 194% rise in the anti-inflammatory marker under a controlled challenge. **Moderate** for mood and anxiety with regular practice | [Activating breathwork](/evidence/activating-breathwork) |

**This is the only family in the library with a serious safety section, and it is not decorative.** Never practise these in or near water, or before swimming — lowered carbon dioxide delays the urge to breathe and lets oxygen fall further before the body objects, which is the specific mechanism by which hyperventilation drowns people. The full cautions are on that page and should be read before practising.

## What the evidence does not support

**That any one protocol is better than another.** Almost nothing in this area has been tested at the resolution of specific counts and sequences. One trial comparing three breathing protocols found the long-exhale practice performed best, and it is a single small exploratory study.

**That the physiology explains the mood benefit.** The common account — slow breathing raises vagal tone, which lifts mood — did not hold in the one trial that measured both: mood improved and heart rate variability did not change in any group. The physiology is well evidenced. The mood effect is well evidenced. The bridge between them is not.

**That the activating form beats the calming one.** In the trial that compared them directly, 21 days of rapid breathing with cold exposure and 21 days of slow breathing with warm showers produced the **same substantial improvement** in depression, anxiety and stress. Both are in this library. The evidence says they are equivalent routes, not that one is superior — so choose the one you will actually keep doing.

**That a device adds anything.** No study has shown that heart-rate feedback equipment outperforms counting. For some people, watching the number becomes its own source of anxiety.

## Which practices sit where

The Breath lever already sorts itself this way, into **Calming** and **The Activators**. This family simply names why that split exists and extends it to the practices outside that lever.

**Calming the body**
Alternate-Nostril Breathing · Deep Belly Breathing · Coherent Breathing · The Physiological Sigh · 4-7-8 Breathing · Progressive Muscle Relaxation · The Corpse Pose · Non-Sleep Deep Rest · Heart Coherence

**Activating the body**
Bellows Breath · Skull-Shining Breath · Cyclic Hyperventilation

**Either, depending on how they are done**
Chanting · Humming · Spinal Breathing · Breath Retention — the first three lengthen the out-breath and so tend to calm, but pace and volume change the effect. Breath Retention depends entirely on what precedes it.

## How much you need to do

- **Five minutes a day** of slow breathing beat meditation on mood over 28 days, and the benefit grew with the number of days practised.
- **Fifteen to twenty minutes a day over about four weeks** is the dose in the blood-pressure trials.
- **Ten to twenty minutes daily** is what the progressive muscle relaxation trials used; one 20-minute pass produced measurable acute change.
- **Around fifteen minutes each morning for three weeks** is the dose behind the strongest mood outcome for activating breathwork.
- Physiological effects of slow breathing appear **immediately**, during practice and just after, at around six breaths a minute.

**So: five to twenty minutes most days, for about a month, is the range the evidence covers** — five minutes for mood, closer to twenty if blood pressure is the target. There is no established optimum, and anyone specifying a precise required dose is going beyond what is known.
