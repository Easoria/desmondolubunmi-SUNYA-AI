# Progressive muscle relaxation and stillness

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for reducing anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms and improving subjective sleep quality in clinical settings, **Preliminary** for long-term blood pressure reduction and standalone physical stillness.

**Traditional convergence — Strong** *(deliberate supine physical release and motionless bodily stillness prescribed independently in Yogic and Daoist contemplative texts for centuries)*.

> **In short:** systematically tensing and abruptly releasing major muscle groups — or settling into deliberate physical stillness — lowers anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms across dozens of trials[^1] and improves self-rated sleep quality.[^2] In hypertension trials, progressive muscle relaxation also trimmed systolic blood pressure by about 7.5 mmHg inside three months, though study quality was rated very low and gains appeared to fade over time.[^3] Two surprises keep this grounded: first, trying to relax harder in bed can backfire in chronic insomnia if it keeps you lying awake;[^6] second, a real subset of chronically anxious people experience a paradoxical spike in anxiety when letting muscular tension go.[^7]

---

## What it is

Two ways of lowering baseline physical bracing and motor arousal:

- **Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR)** — working sequentially from hands and face to feet (or feet to head), lightly tensing one muscle group for several seconds, noticing the contraction, and letting it drop completely while noticing the contrast of release.[^9]
- **Deliberate physical stillness** — lying flat or sitting upright without fidgeting, scratching or shifting, allowing habitual muscular holding to quiet without forcing sleep or deep breathing.[^10]

Both rest on a straightforward observation: most people carry background muscular bracing without noticing it, and intentionally creating contrast or holding stillness reveals where tension is sitting.[^9]

---

## What it does for you

### It eases anxiety, stress and low mood — especially alongside other practices

A 2024 systematic review pooling **46 publications across 16 countries (more than 3,400 adults)** concluded that progressive muscle relaxation reduces stress, anxiety and depression in adults, with **consistently stronger results when combined with other practices** such as breathing, music or imagery than when used completely alone.[^1]

Earlier pooling of **27 relaxation-training studies** (covering progressive relaxation, applied relaxation, autogenic training and meditation) found medium-to-large reductions in anxiety — **d = 0.57** within groups and **d = 0.51** against controls — with larger effects in longer programmes.[^4]

In a single-session laboratory comparison of **60 healthy adults**, **20 minutes** of audio-guided progressive muscle relaxation, deep breathing or guided imagery each raised psychological relaxation states significantly above a control condition while autonomic measures (heart rate and skin conductance) were tracked continuously.[^5]

### It improves self-rated sleep quality in clinical and adult samples

A 2026 meta-analysis of **31 randomised controlled trials (2,277 adults)** found that progressive muscle relaxation improved subjective sleep quality compared with controls (**SMD = −1.74**, improving Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores by **3.79 points**, 95% CI [−4.78, −2.79]), lowered **anxiety (SMD = −1.11)** and improved **quality of life (SMD = 1.32)** across intervention durations.[^2]

### It lowers high blood pressure in the short term

In a 2025 network meta-analysis of **182 studies** on relaxation for hypertension and prehypertension, pooling short-term trials (three months or less) showed that progressive muscle relaxation reduced **systolic blood pressure by 7.46 mmHg** (95% credible interval [−12.15, −2.96]) compared with passive waitlists or usual care — roughly on par with breathing control (**−6.65 mmHg**) and meditation (**−7.71 mmHg**).[^3]

---

## How much you need to do

- **One 20-minute guided pass** was enough to produce measurable acute relaxation in healthy adults.[^5]
- **One slow pass through major muscle groups** (tensing each area for roughly 5 to 10 seconds at moderate effort, then releasing for 20 to 30 seconds) takes **10 to 20 minutes** daily.[^5]
- **Consistent multi-week practice** outperformed brief one-offs across anxiety meta-analyses,[^4] and combining muscle release with slow breathing or calm attention yielded larger mood gains than isolated PMR.[^1]

**So: 10 to 20 minutes once a day — or a quick shoulder, jaw and hand release during high-tension afternoons — covers what the trials tested.**

## Where the evidence stops

**Heterogeneity and low study certainty limit the headline numbers.** In the 31-trial sleep meta-analysis, trials disagreed dramatically (**I² = 83% to 94%**) and leaned heavily on hospitalised or clinical samples who started with poor sleep, meaning everyday gains in healthy sleepers are likely smaller.[^2] In the 182-study blood-pressure network meta-analysis, **evidence certainty was graded very low** due to bias, imprecision and possible publication bias — and blood-pressure drops tended to wane after three months.[^3]

**Relaxing in bed can backfire for chronic insomnia.** In a component network meta-analysis of **241 chronic insomnia trials (31,452 adults)**, relaxation procedures were flagged as **potentially counterproductive for insomnia remission (iOR = 0.81**, 95% CI [0.64, 1.02]) — likely because turning relaxation into an effortful bedtime technique keeps people lying awake in bed instead of following stimulus control.[^6]

**Standalone physical stillness is rarely tested separately.** Outside progressive muscle relaxation scripts and multi-component meditation or rest programmes, pure motionless stillness without breathing or cognitive cues has almost no isolated clinical trial literature.

**Unknowns:** whether classic slow Jacobson training (teaching subtle tension awareness without heavy squeezing) performs better than quick 10-minute audio scripts;[^9] how long sleep-quality gains persist once audio sessions stop;[^2] and which parts of blood-pressure change reflect quiet rest versus regular therapist contact.[^3]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that tensing muscles as hard as possible produces deeper release, or that forcing your body to stay flat and still in bed will knock you out when you are wide awake. Moderate tension works without cramping, and staying in bed doing repeated relaxation passes when alert works against sleep conditioning.[^6]

---

## Who should be careful

**Relaxation-induced anxiety.** Letting go of physical bracing does not calm everyone. Classic clinical experiments showed that a notable subgroup of chronically anxious people experience **paradoxical spikes in anxiety** during progressive relaxation.[^7] Later controlled testing across adults with generalised anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder found this response was driven by **negative contrast sensitivity** — discomfort with feeling calm because dropping your guard makes any subsequent emotional drop feel sharper.[^8] If releasing tension makes you feel panicky, shaky or exposed, open your eyes, practise sitting upright rather than lying flat, or shift to light walking first.

**Joint pain, muscle cramps and acute injury.** *As a practical judgement rather than a trial finding:* never tense a cramped, inflamed, recently injured or post-surgical muscle group. Use gentle mental release on sore areas or skip them and focus on uninjured regions instead.

**Chronic insomnia.** If you use progressive muscle relaxation to unwind before bed and are still wide awake at the end of one pass, follow stimulus control rather than starting a second round in bed: get up into dim light until genuinely sleepy.[^6]

## Traditional convergence

Strong. Two independent contemplative traditions preserved physical stillness and supine muscular release not as passive laziness, but as deliberate methods for dropping subtle agitation.

In India, the 15th-century *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* sets out Corpse Pose (*śavāsana*) in plain physiological language: *"lying supine on the ground like a corpse removes fatigue and gives rest to the mind."*[^10] In China, classical Daoist texts described both sitting stillness and letting go of bodily holding centuries earlier: the 4th-century BCE *Neiye* (*Inward Training*) pairs a *"calm and unmoving"* body with discarding myriad disturbances, while Chapter 6 of the *Zhuangzi* illustrates sitting and forgetting (*zuowang*) as letting limbs and torso drop away alongside strained sensory focus.[^10]

You can test the physical hinge in five minutes: sit comfortably, hunch your shoulders toward your ears and clench both fists at half effort for six seconds, let them drop dead-weight on an exhale, and notice for thirty seconds where else in your jaw, forehead or stomach you are still bracing without realising it.
