# Work and values congruence

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for associations between value fit, meaningful work, engagement and job satisfaction and for job crafting interventions, **Preliminary** for causal mental health outcomes of career change and civilian occupational moral injury.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(aligning one's livelihood with ethics and individual temperament appears independently across Buddhist, Stoic and Indian traditions for over two thousand years)*.

> **In short:** working in a role and organisation whose values and demands fit you predicts higher job satisfaction, lower strain and lower intent to leave across nearly two hundred studies.[^1] Meaningful work tracks moderately with life satisfaction and general health beyond work itself,[^2] and proactively reshaping parts of your own role ("job crafting") raises work engagement in intervention trials.[^6] Two sharp limits keep this grounded: meaningful work does *not* protect against anxiety or stress if day-to-day job conditions are poor,[^4] and treating work as a sacred calling can make people accept overwork, low pay and exploitation.[^8]

---

## What it is

How closely your paid or unpaid daily work matches what you care about, what you are good at, and what you can ethically stand behind. Research separates three layers that are often blurred together:

- **Person–organisation value fit** — whether an organisation's actual priorities and culture match your personal values.[^1]
- **Person–job fit** — whether the daily tasks fit your skills and abilities, and whether the role supplies what you need (autonomy, social contact, income, pace).[^1]
- **Meaningful work and moral alignment** — whether the work feels worthwhile[^2] without requiring you to cross your own ethical boundaries.[^5]

Because most adults spend roughly half their waking weekdays working, chronic mismatch at work functions as an ongoing structural stressor rather than an occasional bad day.[^1]

## What it does for you

### Value and job fit track strongly with satisfaction and staying put

A meta-analysis of **172 studies (836 effect sizes)** found consistent links between workplace fit and how people fare at work:[^1]

| Type of fit | Job satisfaction (*r*) | Organisational commitment (*r*) | Intent to quit (*r*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Person–job fit** | **.56** | **.47** | **−.46** |
| **Person–organisation (values) fit** | **.44** | **.51** | **−.35** |

Good person–job and person–organisation fit were also associated with lower work strain and stronger citizenship behaviour and performance.[^1]

### Meaningful work spills over into general health and life satisfaction

Across **44 studies covering 23,144 workers**, experiencing work as meaningful had large correlations (**r = 0.70+**) with work engagement, commitment and job satisfaction, moderate-to-large correlations (**r = 0.44 to −0.49**) with **life satisfaction, sense of meaning in life, general health and lower withdrawal intentions**, and small-to-moderate correlations (**r = −0.19 to 0.33**) with lower negative affect and self-rated performance.[^2]

A **201-study meta-analysis** of calling orientations found a similar pattern: viewing work as a calling correlated strongly with work meaningfulness (**r = 0.61**, 25 studies), work engagement (**r = 0.49**, 31 studies) and job satisfaction (**r = 0.46**, 64 studies), showing tighter ties to purpose than to daily pleasure, and stronger links to work well-being than overall life satisfaction.[^3]

### Reshaping your current role improves engagement without changing jobs

Because most people cannot quit tomorrow, **job crafting** — proactively adjusting task boundaries, working relationships and cognitive framing inside an existing role — has been tested directly in intervention trials. Across **14 intervention studies**, job crafting training produced small-to-moderate gains in job crafting behaviour (**g = 0.26**, 95% CI [0.11, 0.40]), reducing hindering work demands (**g = 0.44**), **work engagement (g = 0.31**, 95% CI [0.14, 0.50])** and contextual performance (**g = 0.39**).[^6]

A separate meta-analysis restricted to longitudinal, daily-diary and experimental studies confirmed that job crafting predicted higher subsequent work engagement (**d = 0.37**, 95% CI [0.16, 0.58]), while calling for larger randomised controlled trials.[^7]

### Deep moral violation at work carries clinical risk

When work repeatedly requires people to perform, witness or go along with actions that violate deeply held moral beliefs — studied as **potentially morally injurious experiences** — the cost extends beyond job dissatisfaction. Across **13 studies (6,373 participants)**, occupational moral injury accounted for **9.4%** of the variance in post-traumatic stress symptoms, **5.2%** in depression and **2.0%** in suicidality.[^5]

## How much you need to do

- **Audit the specific mismatch before quitting.** Separate whether the problem is *tasks* (person–job fit), *culture and ethics* (person–organisation fit), or *pay and workload* — each calls for a different fix.[^1]
- **Craft both personal and organisational aims inside the role.** In the 14-trial meta-analysis, job crafting interventions boosted work engagement most reliably when workers formed plans linking their own goals to organisational objectives rather than altering tasks in isolation.[^6]
- **Do not rely on meaning to substitute for workable conditions.** Meaning and day-to-day job satisfaction interact: meaningful work still leaves anxiety and stress high if the work itself is miserable.[^4]

**So: clarify whether the gap is task fit, workload, or an ethical conflict; test targeted adjustments inside your current role first when conditions allow, and plan a structured exit rather than self-blame when core values are repeatedly violated.**

## Where the evidence stops

**Most fit and meaning research is correlational.** The large correlations for workplace fit[^1] and meaningful work[^2] come overwhelmingly from observational and cross-sectional surveys, where happier or less depressed workers also tend to perceive better fit. In that 172-study synthesis, subjective perceived fit predicted attitudes far more strongly than objective measured fit — meaning mood and perception inflate part of the correlation.[^1]

**Meaningful work alone does not protect against anxiety or stress.** In a large working sample controlling for job satisfaction, meaningful work still correlated negatively with depression, **but had no significant relationship with anxiety or stress**.[^4] Only workers whose jobs were *both* meaningful and satisfying reported lower anxiety and stress — meaning cannot compensate for abusive management, chronic overload or financial insecurity.[^4]

**Job crafting training has narrow limits.** In the 14-study intervention meta-analysis, task performance improved significantly **only among healthcare employees**, not across general occupations,[^6] and longitudinal reviewers noted how few large, rigorous randomised trials exist.[^7]

**Moral injury research is still mostly military.** Eleven of the thirteen studies in the occupational moral injury meta-analysis examined military samples, leaving civilian workplace prevalence and recovery interventions far less settled.[^5]

**Unknowns:** how long job crafting gains last beyond several months;[^6] whether leaving a mismatched job improves mental health more reliably when income stays stable; and which workplace reforms best reduce moral injury in civilian care and corporate settings.[^5]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that finding your "passion" or "calling" guarantees well-being, or that anyone miserable at work simply needs a grander sense of purpose. In a classic study of zookeepers, a deep sense of calling gave meaning *and* made workers willing to sacrifice pay and personal time while making them vulnerable to management exploitation.[^8]

---

## Who should be careful

**Overwork and self-exploitation in "calling" professions.** In helping, creative, healthcare, teaching and mission-driven work, strong value attachment makes people tolerate chronic underpay, unpaid overtime and poor boundaries.[^8] Watch whether "doing good" has become an excuse for neglecting sleep, relationships or fair compensation.

**Impulsive quitting under financial instability.** *As a practical judgement rather than a trial finding:* leaving a poorly aligned job with zero cash buffer or fallback income trades a values stressor for acute financial scarcity, which carries heavy depression and anxiety risk of its own. Build runway or line up the next step before exiting unless safety or severe ethical harm demands leaving sooner.

**Persistent moral distress and suicidality.** Where work involves repeated ethical violation, institutional betrayal, severe depression or thoughts of self-harm, treat it as an occupational and clinical risk rather than a personal resilience failure and seek medical or psychological care.[^5]

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Long before organisational psychology measured person–organisation fit, three independent traditions treated honest, temperament-suited work as an explicit condition of steadiness rather than an afterthought.

In early Pali Buddhism, the *Magga-vibhaṅga Sutta* and *Vaṇijjā Sutta* make **right livelihood** (*sammā-ājīva*) a core factor of training — defining it by refusing dishonest or harmful ways of earning a living, explicitly ruling out trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants and poisons.[^9] In Rome, Cicero's *De Officiis* distinguished our common human reason from our individual temperament and our chosen profession, arguing that choosing a role against one's own nature produces inner discord and inauthenticity.[^9] And in India, the *Bhagavad Gītā* states twice that doing one's own work (*svadharma*) suited to one's nature — even imperfectly — is far better and safer than performing someone else's role impeccably.[^9]

You can test the practical boundary directly: write down the specific part of your workweek that leaves you flattest, separate whether it is a *task* mismatch, an *overload* problem or an *ethical* clash, and change one small boundary or task over two weeks to see what actually moves.
