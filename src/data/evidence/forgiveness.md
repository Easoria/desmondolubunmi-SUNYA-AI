# Forgiveness

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for structured process-based forgiveness programmes reducing anger, stress, depression and anxiety, **Preliminary** for physical health outcomes and brief decision-only exercises.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(relinquishing resentment while separating inner release from accountability appears independently across Buddhist, Jewish, Christian and Stoic texts for over two millennia)*.

> **In short:** working steadily through structured forgiveness exercises reduces anger, stress, depression and anxiety by a small-to-moderate amount across clinical and community trials.[^2] Brief "decide to let it go" workshops fail to move the needle: one-to-two-hour decision-only sessions produced no measurable benefit over controls, while multi-week process programmes did.[^3] And one boundary separates healthy forgiveness from self-harm: releasing resentment is *not* the same as trusting again or tolerating ongoing mistreatment. In longitudinal marriage studies, forgiving a repeatedly aggressive or unrepentant partner predicted continued aggression[^7] and eroded the victim's own self-respect.[^8]

---

## What it is

Deliberately letting go of resentment, hostility and the wish for retaliation after being hurt, without excusing the wrong, forgetting what happened, or automatically reconciling with the person who caused it.[^1]

Researchers separate two parts that get confused in everyday speech:

- **Decisional forgiveness** — choosing not to seek revenge or hold onto retaliation as a goal.[^3]
- **Emotional forgiveness** — gradually replacing chronic anger, bitterness and hurt with steadier, neutral or compassionate feelings through sustained reflection.[^3]

Reconciliation is a third, separate decision — one that depends on safety, honesty and changed behaviour from the other person.[^8]

---

## What it does for you

### Structured forgiveness training reduces anger, depression and anxiety

Across **54 empirical reports (2,323 participants)**, structured forgiveness interventions increased forgiveness moderately compared with no-treatment controls (**d = 0.56**, 95% CI [0.43, 0.68]) and outperformed alternative active treatments (**d = 0.45**, 95% CI [0.21, 0.69]).[^1] Those programmes also produced small-to-large psychological benefits alongside forgiveness itself: **less depression (d = 0.34)**, **less anxiety (d = 0.63)** and **greater hope (d = 1.00)**.[^1]

A meta-analysis focused on **15 randomised and controlled trials (860 adults and adolescents)** hurt by interpersonal conflict, abuse or divorce found similar emotional shifts from process-based forgiveness programmes:[^2]

| Outcome | Effect size (SMD) |
|---|---|
| Stress and distress | **−0.66** |
| Anger and hostility | **−0.49** |
| Depression | **−0.37** |

### Rehearsing grudges keeps physiological stress elevated

In a controlled laboratory experiment with **71 adults**, participants repeatedly imagined a real person who had hurt them under two conditions: rehearsing the hurt and nursing the grudge versus adopting empathy and forgiveness.[^4] Nursing unforgiving thoughts raised **blood pressure, heart rate, skin conductance and brow muscle tension** significantly above forgiving imagery — and elevated physiological arousal lingered into the recovery period after the imagery stopped.[^4]

### Drops in perceived stress precede better health ratings

In a five-week longitudinal study tracking **148 young adults**, increases in forgiveness predicted subsequent drops in **perceived stress**, which in turn predicted fewer mental and physical health symptoms — whereas initial symptom changes did not predict forgiveness.[^5] Across **128 independent samples (58,531 participants)**, trait forgiveness also showed a modest positive correlation with self-reported physical health (**r = .14**, 95% CI [.11, .16]).[^6]

---

## How much you need to do

- **One-to-two-hour "decide to forgive" workshops failed** to outperform controls in a nine-study synthesis.[^3]
- **Hours invested strongly predicted improvement** across the 54-report meta-analysis: longer multi-week courses beat short workshops, and one-to-one therapy outperformed group sessions.[^1]
- **Multi-week structured process models** — naming the hurt honestly, understanding its impact, deciding not to retaliate, reframing the offender's context without excusing the harm, and sitting with grief — produced moderate gains in groups (**d = 0.82** on forgiveness, **d = 0.59** on emotional health) and large gains in individual sessions (**d = 1.66** and **d = 1.42**).[^3]

**So: expect forgiveness to take weeks of structured reflection rather than a single decision.** Treat it as a stepped process that starts with honesty about what was wrong before attempting to release anger.[^3]

## Where the evidence stops

**Physical health claims rely mostly on cross-sectional self-reports.** The 128-sample correlation between forgiveness and physical health (**r = .14**) is largely cross-sectional and based on self-rated symptoms rather than clinical disease endpoints or randomised long-term health trials.[^6]

**Long-term follow-up in clinical trials is thin.** The 15-trial review of interpersonal trauma and conflict noted high heterogeneity across trials and limited long-term follow-up data beyond the end of therapy.[^2] Most participants across trials are university students or self-selected volunteers who want to forgive, rather than severe clinical populations.[^1]

**Forgiving an unrepentant or aggressive partner backfires.** Two longitudinal studies expose the danger of treating forgiveness as unconditionally good regardless of context:

- In **135 newlywed couples** tracked over four years, **more frequent forgiveness predicted stable or growing partner psychological and physical aggression** when spouses were frequently aggressive, whereas spouses who were less quick to forgive saw partner aggression decline over time.[^7]
- Across two longitudinal studies and experiments on marriage, forgiving **eroded the victim's self-respect and clarity of identity** whenever the offending partner failed to make sincere amends — lifting self-respect only when partners showed genuine accountability.[^8]

**Unknowns:** whether self-guided forgiveness workbooks match therapist-led courses in severe betrayal;[^3] how well gains hold over one to five years;[^2] and whether reducing grudge rehearsal changes long-term cardiovascular risk outside brief lab measurements.[^4]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that forgiving means pretending an injury was small, dropping necessary boundaries, or staying in an unsafe relationship to "keep the peace." Letting go of toxic anger inside yourself and letting an untrustworthy person back into your life are two completely separate decisions.[^8]

---

## Who should be careful

**Ongoing abuse, domestic violence or chronic exploitation.** Never treat forgiveness as a reason to tolerate continuing harm, drop legal or physical boundaries, or stay with an aggressive partner who refuses accountability.[^7] Releasing personal bitterness does not require resuming trust.[^8]

**Rushing past justified anger or trauma.** Brief "choose to forgive today" pressures fail in trials[^3] and can leave people feeling invalidated or guilty for still hurting. *As a practical judgement:* acknowledge the full reality and injustice of what happened first; where abuse or post-traumatic stress is involved, work through forgiveness with a qualified trauma-informed therapist rather than self-help prompts.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Four independent traditions concluded over two millennia ago that nursing hatred harms the person carrying it — while explicitly separating inner non-retaliation from cheap reconciliation without amends.

In early Buddhism, the *Dhammapada* states that rehearsing *"he abused me, he struck me, he robbed me"* keeps enmity alive forever, because hatred never ends through hatred — only through non-enmity — while the *Kakacūpama Sutta* trains steadiness of mind under verbal or physical provocation without retaliation.[^9] In Jewish law and ethics, Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah* draws a sharp institutional line: wrongdoers must first make restitution and ask forgiveness directly before reconciliation applies, while advising the injured party not to stay obstinately cruel once sincere amends are offered.[^9] In the Christian Gospels, *Matthew* refuses to cap forgiveness at seven times while *Luke* explicitly pairs repeated forgiveness with the offender turning back in repentance.[^9] And in Roman Stoicism, Seneca's *On Anger* urges letting go of daily slights not to flatter the offender, but because feeding anger punishes the person holding it.[^9]

You can test the immediate physiological hinge in five minutes: bring to mind a moderate, real irritation from this week, notice your jaw, shoulders and breathing while rehearsing why the person was wrong, then shift for three minutes to viewing the same event simply as something that happened that no longer needs your ongoing tension — and observe whether your physical tightness changes.
