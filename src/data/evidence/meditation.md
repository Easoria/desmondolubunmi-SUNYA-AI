# Meditation — focused attention and open monitoring

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for anxiety, depression and pain; **Moderate to large** in healthy people against no treatment; **Preliminary** for attention at ordinary doses
**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(the central practice of every contemplative tradition, in continuous use for millennia)*

> **In short:** meditation reduces anxiety, depression and pain by a moderate amount, and the benefit is still there months later.[^1] It survives the strictest test in the field — being compared against people doing some other active thing rather than nothing.[^1] In ordinary stressed people rather than patients, the effects are larger again, with stress improving most.[^2] Attention does measurably improve, but at retreat-level practice rather than at twenty minutes a day.[^4] For something free, portable and essentially harmless,[^1] this is among the best-evidenced things a person can do for themselves without a prescription.

---

## What it is

Training attention, in two broad modes that most traditions use together.

**Focused attention** — holding attention on one thing, noticing when it wanders, returning it. The object can be the breath, a point, a sound, a sensation.

**Open monitoring** — holding attention broadly, aware of whatever arises without selecting or following any of it.

The research does not cleanly separate these. Nearly every trialled programme teaches both, so the evidence below covers them together.

---

## What it does for you

### It reduces anxiety, depression and pain — and this holds up under the strictest test

The most rigorous assessment is a review commissioned by the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality and published in *JAMA Internal Medicine*. It covered **41 randomised trials with 2,993 adults**, and made a methodological choice most reviews do not: **it counted only trials with active control groups.**[^1]

That matters. Comparing meditation with a waiting list tells you that doing something beats doing nothing. Comparing it with another activity tells you whether the meditation itself is doing work.

| Outcome | Effect at 8 weeks | At 3–6 months |
|---|---|---|
| Anxiety | 0.40 | 0.22 |
| Depression | 0.32 | 0.23 |
| Pain | 0.33 | — |

Small-to-moderate improvements, **still present three to six months later**.[^1]

In plain terms: most people who take up a structured meditation programme and stay with it become measurably less anxious and less depressed, and stay that way for months. It held up against people doing some other active thing, not against people doing nothing. That is a harder test than almost anything else in this library has passed.

### In ordinary people under stress, the effects are larger

The review above studied only people with a medical or psychiatric condition.[^1] Most people reading this do not have one.

A separate meta-analysis of **29 studies covering 2,668 healthy adults** doing standard eight-week courses found substantially bigger effects: **g = 0.53 between groups**, with **large effects on stress**, moderate effects on anxiety, depression, distress and quality of life, and small effects on burnout.[^2] Gains held at an average of 19 weeks afterwards.[^2]

Both numbers are true and they answer different questions. *Does an eight-week course help an ordinary stressed person more than doing nothing?* — moderately to largely, yes. *Does it help someone with a diagnosis more than exercise or therapy would?* — not demonstrably. The difference is mostly the comparison group: only one of those 29 studies used an active control.[^2]

### Attention does improve — at serious doses

The clinical trials used light doses, typically eight weeks, and the review's authors note that many used low meditation doses.[^1] One study went much further.

The Shamatha Project randomised 60 healthy adults to a **three-month residential retreat with around six hours of practice daily**, against a matched waiting-list group who later did the same retreat.[^3] It is one of the most thoroughly measured meditation studies ever run. Intensive training produced:

- **improved perceptual discrimination and sustained attention**[^4]
- **reduced mind wandering during reading**[^5]
- **improved response inhibition**, which tracked with better emotional functioning[^6]
- **adaptive psychological functioning that was still elevated six months later**[^3]

So the honest statement on attention is not that meditation fails to train it. It is that **eight weeks at twenty minutes a day has not been shown to, and three months at six hours a day has.** That is a dose finding, and for anyone deciding how seriously to practise it is probably the most useful thing on this page.

---

## How much you need to do

- **Eight weeks** is the standard trialled format, and it is enough for the anxiety, depression and pain results.[^1]
- Benefits at that dose **persisted three to six months** after the course ended.[^1]
- Many trials used **low doses**, which the authors note may understate what sustained practice does.[^1]
- **Attention gains required retreat conditions** — three months, around six hours daily.[^3]

**So: eight weeks of daily practice is the dose with outcome evidence behind it.** Expect mood and pain benefits there. Do not expect measurable attention gains at that dose; those appear where practice becomes the main activity of the day.

Partly: Disidentifying · Self-Inquiry · Looking at Death · Being Nothing — these use the same attentional skills, but their specific aims have not been trialled.

## Where the evidence stops

**Meditation has not been shown to work better than other active approaches.** Across 53 comparisons with specific alternatives — exercise, progressive muscle relaxation, cognitive behavioural therapy, medication — only two significantly favoured meditation, and **five significantly favoured the comparison treatment**.[^1] The authors state plainly there was no evidence meditation programmes were better than any active treatment.[^1]

This is not a reason to stop meditating. It is a reason to describe it accurately: meditation is one effective option among several, not a superior one. Someone who would rather run than sit is not settling for less.

**Where the evidence is weak or absent in that review:** low evidence of improvement for stress and distress; low evidence of *no* effect on positive mood; insufficient evidence on sleep and substance use; insufficient evidence on attention, where only one trial of 21 caregivers measured it and found nothing.[^1]

**The retreat findings do not transfer to practice at home.** Sixty experienced, self-selected people at one centre with one teacher, with many published analyses drawn from a single dataset, is not a general result.

**Nothing has been studied about the deeper aims of contemplative practice.** No trial has examined self-inquiry, non-dual recognition, or the dissolution of the sense of a separate self. These are not disproven; they have not been tested.

**A note that reconciles two findings.** This review found mantra programmes showed low evidence of no effect, or insufficient evidence, across all outcomes[^1] — apparently contradicting the large mantra meta-analysis under *Sustained vocalisation*, where effects were moderate and consistent. Both are correct, and the difference is the control group: the mantra review included comparisons against no treatment, this one required an active comparison. The same pattern appeared there directly — against ordinary relaxation, the mantra advantage disappeared. The consistent reading across both: **these practices beat doing nothing, and have not been shown to beat other active alternatives.**

## Who should be careful

Only nine of the 41 trials reported on harms at all, and none found any.[^1] Reassuring but thin — absence of reported harm is not evidence of safety.

Outside this review, difficult experiences during meditation are documented directly. A mixed-methods study interviewed nearly 100 meditators and teachers across the Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan traditions and found that **all of them reported multiple unexpected experiences**, mapped into a taxonomy of 59 experiences across seven domains — cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative, sense of self and social.[^7] Reported examples included hypersensitivity to light or sound, insomnia, involuntary movement, and fear, anxiety, panic or loss of emotion; durations ranged from days to over a decade.[^7]

Two things to hold together. That study deliberately sought out difficult experiences and **did not estimate how common they are**, so it is not a base rate.[^7] But it establishes that these experiences are real, varied, and sometimes long-lasting rather than anecdotal.

If practice reliably makes you more distressed rather than less, that is information, not failure. Stop, and talk to someone qualified.

---

## Traditional convergence

Very strong, and the broadest in the framework. Attention training is the central practice of Buddhist *samatha* and *vipassana*, Hindu *dharana* and *dhyana*, Christian contemplative prayer and the Desert Fathers' watchfulness, Sufi *muraqaba*, and Jewish *hitbodedut*.[^8]

These traditions disagree about nearly everything — what a person is, what the world is, what the practice is ultimately for. They agree on sitting still and training attention.
