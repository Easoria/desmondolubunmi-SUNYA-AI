# Slow and paced breathing

*Reviewed November 2026*

*Supersedes the earlier separate records for "slow breathing and extended exhale" and "paced breathing at resonance frequency" — the research does not separate them, so neither should we.*

**Research strength — Strong** for blood pressure and physiology, **Moderate** for mood.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(independently arrived at by unrelated traditions, and two of them have been measured producing the same rate)*.

> **In short:** slowing the breath to around six a minute lowers blood pressure by enough to matter clinically — the authors of that meta-analysis concluded it "may be a reasonable first treatment" for low-risk people with hypertension or prehypertension who are reluctant to start medication.[^7] It shifts the nervous system measurably toward rest and recovery, across one of the largest evidence bases in this framework.[^1] And it reduces stress, anxiety and low mood by a small-to-moderate amount,[^2] with the benefit growing the longer you keep it up.[^6] It costs nothing, needs no equipment, and has essentially no side effects.[^1]

---

## What it is

Deliberately slowing the breath. Two things can be varied, and they are independent:

**Rate** — how many breaths a minute. Slow usually means around six. At roughly this rate the heart rate, blood pressure and breathing rhythms line up and reinforce each other, which is called resonance; this synchronisation has been measured directly at six breaths a minute, along with a significant rise in baroreflex sensitivity.[^4] The rate at which it is strongest varies slightly between individuals.[^1]

**Ratio** — how much longer the out-breath is than the in-breath. Coherent breathing is five seconds in, five out: slow rate, no exhale emphasis. The physiological sigh and 4-7-8 are mostly about the ratio.

You can change either without the other, and one trial comparing them head to head found they did not perform identically.[^6]

---

## What it does for you

### It lowers blood pressure

Pooling **17 trials**, slow breathing reduced blood pressure by **5.62 mmHg systolic** (95% CI [−7.86, −3.38]) and **2.97 mmHg diastolic** (95% CI [−4.28, −1.66]).[^7]

Two things make this the most important finding on the page. First, blood pressure is measured with a cuff rather than reported on a questionnaire, so expectation cannot inflate it. Second, a reduction of this size is clinically meaningful — the authors conclude slow breathing "may be a reasonable first treatment" for low-risk people with hypertension or prehypertension who are reluctant to start medication.[^7]

And the unassisted version did at least as well as the machine. Device-guided breathing gave −5.28/−2.67 mmHg; **non-device-guided pranayama gave −8.45/−6.90 mmHg**.[^7] Only two trials used the unassisted form, so that larger figure is not firm — but nothing here suggests you need equipment.

### It shifts the nervous system toward rest and recovery

The largest review in this area pooled **223 studies** and found that voluntary slow breathing increases parasympathetic activity — the branch of the nervous system associated with rest and recovery.[^1] The effect appears **during practice, immediately after, and after sustained training**, which is to say it is both an acute tool and a trainable change.[^1] The authors describe it as low-cost with few expected adverse effects.[^1]

This is among the best-supported material anywhere in Sunya.

### It improves the body's blood-pressure regulation reflex

Breathing at around six a minute produces large synchronised swings in heart rate and blood pressure, and raises baroreflex sensitivity measurably[^4] — the reflex by which the body corrects its own blood pressure moment to moment.

### It reduces stress, anxiety and low mood

Across **12 randomised controlled trials covering 785 people**, breathing practices reduced stress by a small-to-moderate amount (g = −0.35, 95% CI [−0.55, −0.14]), with anxiety and low mood improving by similar margins.[^2] For depressive symptoms specifically, **14 randomised trials of 794 adults** give g = 0.38 (95% CI [0.16, 0.60]).[^5]

Small-to-moderate is where almost everything genuinely useful in this field sits. It means most people who practise consistently notice something.

### And the benefit accumulates

In a 28-day trial, five minutes of daily breathing improved mood **more than mindfulness meditation did**, and the improvement **grew with the number of days practised**.[^6]

That is worth dwelling on. Most things that make you feel better work once and then stop working. This got better the longer it went on.

---

## Does the ratio matter, or just the rate?

The clearest comparison available. In that same trial, three protocols ran side by side: cyclic sighing (a long-exhale practice), box breathing (equal counts, no exhale emphasis) and cyclic hyperventilation. **Cyclic sighing performed best.**[^6]

That is the one piece of direct evidence that the extended out-breath does something the slow rate alone does not.[^6] It is a single, small, exploratory study and should not be leaned on hard — but it points where the traditions also point, and it is the reason the physiological sigh is worth distinguishing from box breathing rather than treating all slow breathing as one thing.

---

## How much you need to do

- **Five minutes a day was enough** to beat meditation on mood over 28 days, self-administered at home from a video.[^6]
- **Blood pressure trials typically ran 15 to 20 minutes a day over about four weeks.**[^7]
- **Physiological effects appear immediately**, during practice and just after, at around six breaths a minute.[^1]
- Most mood studies used **a few weeks of regular practice**.[^2]
- Biofeedback training ranged from one session to fifty, and **no dose-response relationship was found** — more sessions did not produce better outcomes.[^3]

**So: five to twenty minutes a day, most days, for about a month is the range the evidence covers** — five minutes for mood, closer to fifteen or twenty if blood pressure is the target. There is no established optimum, and anyone specifying a precise required dose is going beyond what is known.

## Where the evidence stops

**The link between the physiology and the mood is assumed, not demonstrated.** The common account — slow breathing raises vagal tone, raised vagal tone lifts mood — did not hold in the one trial that tested both: mood improved and heart rate variability did not change in any group.[^6] Some of the mood benefit may come from attention, from expectation, or simply from taking five minutes. The physiology is well evidenced. The mood effect is well evidenced. The bridge between them is not.

**The largest published mood figures are inflated by weak design.** Biofeedback reviews report a large effect (g = 0.83) across 24 studies, but only one of those was at low risk of bias, every outcome was self-reported, and the authors note expectancy may have contributed.[^3] The better-controlled pools give roughly half that.[^5] **The honest reading is moderate, not large.**

**A new trial could find nothing.** The depression analysis reports a prediction interval of −0.19 to 0.96[^5] — what to expect of the *next* study rather than an average of old ones — and it includes zero. The average is positive and real; confidence about any particular setting is lower than the headline implies.

**It does not appear to change sleep architecture.** A small crossover trial measured 20 adults with overnight polysomnography after 30 minutes of five-a-minute breathing before bed. Total sleep time, time to fall asleep and sleep stages were all unchanged.[^8] Twenty people is a pilot, not a verdict — but slow breathing before bed should not be sold as a sleep fix.

**Nothing here runs beyond a few weeks.** No study followed people longer.

**Unknowns:** whether an individually-tuned resonance rate beats simply breathing at six a minute; whether a device adds anything over counting; whether it treats any diagnosed condition, since few trials used clinical populations.[^3]

The 28-day trial was registered after data collection began, run remotely with little control over how people practised, and recruited mostly from one undergraduate class. Its senior author declares a commercial relationship with the wearable manufacturer used.[^6] Useful; not definitive.

## Who should be careful

Among the safest interventions available, and suitable for almost everyone. The largest review of the physiology describes few expected adverse effects.[^1]

*The three cautions below are practical judgements rather than trial findings — none of the studies cited on this page tested them. They are marked as such so you can weigh them accordingly.*

**Panic and close attention to the breath.** Some people with panic disorder find that attending closely to breathing raises anxiety rather than lowering it. If that happens, stop, and use a practice that directs attention outward instead.

**Monitoring devices.** Devices that display heart rate can themselves become a source of anxiety for people prone to watching their bodies closely. If checking the number becomes the point, drop the device and count.

Slow breathing is not a treatment for any respiratory or cardiac condition and does not replace anything a doctor has told you to do. If you are on blood-pressure medication, do not change it on the strength of a breathing practice.

This entry covers slow breathing only. Rapid, forceful breathing and breath holding carry real cautions and are covered separately.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong, and in one case measurable.

Deliberate breath regulation appears independently in Indian pranayama, Chinese qigong and Daoist practice, Tibetan Buddhist training, Christian hesychasm and Sufi practice — in continuous use for centuries, with the lengthened out-breath recurring across all of them.[^9] These traditions had no shared physiology to reason from and mostly no contact with one another.

More concretely: a BMJ study measured 23 adults reciting the Latin Ave Maria of the Catholic rosary, and separately a yoga mantra. **Both, performed as they are traditionally performed, produced a breathing rate of six per minute** — along with synchronised cardiovascular rhythms and a significant rise in baroreflex sensitivity.[^4]

Two traditions, no contact, entirely different purposes, neither attempting to regulate anything physiological, arriving at the same rate. The convergence is on the practice rather than the explanation, and it is the sort of result that makes traditional material worth taking seriously as a source of hypotheses.
