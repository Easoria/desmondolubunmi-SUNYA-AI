# Expressive writing

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for small reductions in everyday distress and reported symptoms, **Preliminary** for clinical depression, trauma and physical disease markers.

**Traditional convergence — Weak** *(the structured emotional-disclosure protocol dates from 1986, though private written self-examination appears earlier in Stoic and Catholic practice)*.

> **In short:** writing privately about an unsettling experience for 15 to 20 minutes across three or four sessions produces a small, measurable improvement in distress and self-reported physical symptoms across hundreds of trials.[^1] Those gains often appear weeks later rather than immediately after writing, and short spacing of one to three days between sessions works better than weekly writing.[^2] The gap between reputation and evidence is wide: the single largest effect is how helpful people *feel* it was, while behaviour barely changes at all.[^1] It does not treat clinical depression on its own,[^3] and for people prone to brooding after a recent loss it can make recovery harder.[^13]

---

## What it is

Writing continuously in private for **15 to 20 minutes** across three or four sessions about your deepest thoughts and feelings around an unresolved or stressful experience.[^4] Grammar, spelling and structure do not matter, and the pages are meant for nobody else to read.[^4]

It differs from keeping a daily diary or a gratitude list in two ways. First, it asks specifically for *both* what happened and the emotions attached to it, rather than factual recording or positive reframing.[^4] Second, it is used as a brief, bounded protocol rather than an open-ended daily habit.[^4]

---

## What it does for you

### It produces a small overall improvement — largest in how helpful it feels

The largest synthesis in this field pooled **146 randomised studies covering 10,994 participants** and found a statistically significant, small overall effect (**r = .075**, roughly *d* = 0.15).[^1] Looking at the categories separately shows where the effect actually sits:[^1]

| Outcome category | Effect size (mean *r*) |
|---|---|
| Subjective impact (how much people felt it helped) | **.159** |
| Reported physical health (symptoms, doctor visits) | **.072** |
| Physiological functioning (driven by immune markers) | **.059** |
| Psychological health (distress, mood, anxiety) | **.056** |
| General functioning (work, grades, relationships) | **.046** |
| Health behaviours (exercise, diet, adherence) | .007 *(not significant, p = .44)* |

The pattern is instructive. Feeling that writing helped is nearly three times larger than the measured drop in distress, and writing about a problem did not measurably change what people did about it.[^1]

### Benefits on depression, anxiety and stress tend to show up after a delay

A meta-analysis of **31 randomised controlled trials with follow-up assessments (4,012 participants)** in healthy and subclinical adults found a small, durable reduction in symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress (**Hedges' g = −0.12**, 95% CI [−0.21, −0.04]).[^2]

Those reductions emerged at follow-up rather than immediately after the final session, likely because confronting painful material stirs up short-term upset before the reaction settles.[^2] Sessions spaced **1 to 3 days apart** produced significantly larger gains (**g_diff = −0.18**, *p* = .01) than sessions spaced four to seven days or longer apart.[^2]

### A few trials found striking physical and immune effects

In a randomised trial of **112 outpatients** with asthma or rheumatoid arthritis (107 completing), writing about stressful experiences led to measurable clinical gains four months later.[^5] Asthma patients' predicted lung function (**FEV1**) rose from **63.9% to 76.3%** (*p* < .001) against no change in neutral-writing controls (`64.0% to 65.3%`), and rheumatologist-assessed disease severity in arthritis fell by **28%** (*p* = .001).[^5] Overall, **47.1%** of expressive writers showed clinically relevant improvement compared with **24.3%** of controls.[^5]

Two smaller physiological trials pointed in the same direction. Forty medical students who wrote about personal trauma before a **hepatitis B vaccination** produced significantly higher antibody levels at one, four and six months than controls, though circulating T-helper cells dipped briefly right after writing.[^6] And in older adults aged 64 to 97, **76%** of small skin-biopsy wounds had healed by day 11 after expressive writing compared with **42%** in a daily-activities writing control, even though perceived stress did not differ between groups.[^7]

---

## How much you need to do

- **15 to 20 minutes per session, for at least 3 or 4 sessions** is the standard protocol.[^4] Trials using at least three sessions of at least 15 minutes produced larger effects than briefer doses.[^1]
- **Space sessions 1 to 3 days apart** — consecutive days or every other day beat weekly spacing in follow-up trials.[^2]
- **Write privately, for yourself alone.** Studies in which writing was unsubmitted and done in private produced larger benefits than writing meant to be read or handed in.[^1]
- **Focus on an unresolved or recent issue** rather than a distant event you have already made peace with.[^1]

**So: 15 to 20 minutes on three or four consecutive or alternate days, kept private, is the dose the research supports.** Use it as a short, occasional intervention when something is stuck, rather than forcing it every day.

## Where the evidence stops

**The average effect has shrunk as better-controlled trials accumulated.** Earlier reviews reported moderate gains (*d* = 0.47, *r* = .23), whereas the 146-trial synthesis including unpublished studies dropped to *r* = .075,[^1] and the 31-trial follow-up analysis sits at *g* = −0.12.[^2] That is a genuine effect, and it is small — easy to miss from week to week.

**It has not been shown to treat clinical depression.** A meta-analysis of **39 randomised trials (4,009 participants)** found no significant long-term reduction in depressive symptoms across physically healthy adults (**g = −0.03**, 95% CI [−0.09, 0.03]).[^3]

**It has repeatedly failed in severe medical illness and disaster trauma.** A meta-analysis of **16 randomised trials in cancer patients** found no significant benefit for psychological health (*g* = 0.04, *p* = 0.42), physical health or quality of life.[^8] A nationwide trial of **507 breast cancer survivors** found no overall differences in physical symptoms or GP visits over nine months.[^9] In the largest trial of expressive writing to date — **1,090 perinatal women** affected by Hurricane Harvey — expressive writing failed to reduce post-traumatic stress and was linked to *worse* distress at two months among women who experienced the writing negatively.[^10] Across trauma studies more broadly, within-group drops in post-traumatic stress are small and gains in quality of life are negligible to small.[^11]

**Physical healing findings have not replicated consistently.** A follow-up randomised trial in **76 bariatric surgery patients** found that pre-surgical expressive writing *failed* to increase wound collagen deposition — and patients who wrote neutrally about daily activities actually healed better on that marker (*p* = .020).[^12]

**Unknowns:** why identical instructions help in one group and do nothing in another; how much of any gain comes from emotional exposure versus organising a coherent story; and whether structured or self-compassionate prompts reliably outperform open-ended disclosure.

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that writing about a problem substitutes for doing something about it, or that feeling raw afterwards means deeper healing is underway. Expressive writing did not change health behaviours across 146 trials,[^1] and distress during writing can sometimes predict *worse* outcomes rather than better ones.[^10]

## Who should be careful

**Short-term distress after writing is normal.** Feeling sadder, flatter or more unsettled immediately after a session is a standard acute effect of the protocol.[^4] If that mood lingers or interferes with daily life, stop.

**Recent breakup, bereavement or high brooding.** In a trial of adults recently separated from a spouse, people high in brooding rumination assigned to expressive writing reported *more* emotional disturbance eight months later than controls who wrote about daily schedules.[^13] If writing turns into repetitive looping over what went wrong without shifting perspective, drop it.

**People who rarely express emotion.** In a randomised trial of 116 healthy adults, participants *low* in natural emotional expressiveness showed a significant *increase* in anxiety three months after expressive writing, whereas highly expressive writers improved.[^14] Do not force emotional disclosure if it leaves you feeling exposed or more anxious.

**Active trauma, acute crisis or severe depression.** Unassisted emotional writing is ineffective and can worsen symptoms during acute post-disaster distress.[^10] *As a practical judgement:* if you are dealing with active trauma, severe depression or thoughts of self-harm, do not run this protocol on your own; use professional support instead.

---

## Traditional convergence

Weak. Writing continuously for 15 minutes about a distressing experience as a health practice is modern, originating in psychological experiments in 1986.[^4] For most of history, paper was scarce and personal emotional writing was not an accessible practice for ordinary people.

Earlier traditions did use private written self-examination for a different end: moral and cognitive steadiness rather than emotional discharge. In Stoicism, Epictetus advised students to write down core principles daily to keep them ready under pressure, and Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* (*Ta eis heauton*, "To Himself") survived as private notebooks testing his own judgements during military campaigns.[^15] In Catholic practice, Ignatius of Loyola's *The Spiritual Exercises* instructed practitioners to examine a chosen habit twice each day and mark points along parallel horizontal lines for each day of the week to track change over time.[^16] Those older practices share the move of putting private reflection onto a page to inspect it from a step back, even though their instructions and aims differ from modern expressive writing.
