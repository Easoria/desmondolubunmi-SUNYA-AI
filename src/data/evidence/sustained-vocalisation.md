# Sustained vocalisation — chanting and humming

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for chanting and mantra on mental health, **None found** for the nitric-oxide explanation of humming
**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(independently arrived at by every major contemplative tradition, in continuous use for millennia)*

> **In short:** repeating a sustained sound helps with anxiety, stress, low mood and post-traumatic symptoms by a moderate amount, across one of the largest bodies of evidence in this framework — 51 studies and several thousand people, pointing consistently the same way.[^1] It is also a back door into the best-evidenced mechanism on this site, because chanting slows the breath to around six a minute whether you intend it to or not.[^3] It is free, needs nothing, and is among the safest practices there is.[^1]

---

## What it is

Producing a sustained sound with the voice, repeatedly — chanted aloud, murmured, or hummed with the mouth closed.

Chanting also lengthens the out-breath, so it rests on **slow and paced breathing** as well as on this mechanism. Some of what follows may be the breathing rather than the sound — which, given how well evidenced slow breathing is, is not bad news.

---

## What it does for you

### It reduces anxiety, stress, low mood and post-traumatic symptoms

The most substantial review pooled **51 studies** of mantra-based meditation, and the results are consistent across every outcome measured:[^1]

| Outcome | Studies | People | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PTSD symptoms** | 6 | 513 | **g = −0.59** |
| Anxiety | 25 | 1,825 | g = −0.46 |
| Stress | 14 | 1,078 | g = −0.45 |
| Depression | 22 | 1,616 | g = −0.33 |
| Mental health quality of life | 9 | 592 | g = 0.32 |

Moderate effects, with low to moderate variation between studies — meaning the findings replicate rather than bouncing around.[^1] That is a far larger and more consistent evidence base than most things in this framework have.

The strongest result is for **post-traumatic stress**, which is worth noting because it is also the hardest of these to treat.

### It is a route into the best-evidenced mechanism in the library

Chanting, done the way the traditions do it, produces a breathing rate of about six a minute — this has been measured directly.[^3] At that rate the heart rate and blood pressure rhythms synchronise, and baroreflex sensitivity rises significantly.[^3]

So chanting inherits what slow and paced breathing has behind it, including the blood-pressure reduction. You get there by singing rather than by counting, which for many people is easier to sustain.

### It gives attention something to do

Not a research finding, but worth saying plainly: the practical advantage of chanting over an instruction to relax is that it occupies the mind. "Relax" is difficult to follow. A repeated sound is easy to follow, and you can tell whether you are doing it.

---

## How much you need to do

- Mantra interventions in the review **varied widely** in length and format; the analysis took follow-up points closest to four months where available.[^1] So the evidence covers weeks to months of regular practice rather than single sessions.
- The rate that produces the cardiovascular effect is **around six repetitions a minute**, which traditional chanting arrives at naturally when performed at its usual pace.[^3]
- No study has established a minimum useful dose.

Chanting · Humming

## Where the evidence stops

**No study in the review was rated at low risk of bias.** Most were rated high, and every one carried high or uncertain risk on blinding, because you cannot blind someone to whether they are chanting.[^1] That does not make the findings wrong, but expectation cannot be ruled out as a contributor.

**And it has not been shown to beat ordinary relaxation.** Compared against relaxation rather than against no treatment, the effect on anxiety was not statistically significant (g = −0.18, 95% CI [−0.51, 0.15]).[^1]

Chanting clearly beats doing nothing. Whether it beats lying down quietly is unresolved. The practical case for it over "just relax" is the one above — it is easier to actually do.

**The nitric-oxide story about humming is not supported, and should not be used.** Humming raises nitric oxide in the nasal passages, and this has been widely offered as an explanation for its effects on mental clarity. Tested directly across four experiments with several hundred participants, humming **did not** improve short-term memory, reaction speed or emotional processing.[^2] It made letter recall *worse* than sitting quietly, and slowed responses in a detection task.[^2] The authors conclude there is no evidence it improves short-term cognitive performance, and suggest nasal nitric oxide either does not reach the brain in meaningful amounts or does not do this in humans.[^2]

Humming is still worth doing — it lengthens the exhale, it is felt in the face and skull, it is quiet and easy. Just not for that reason. Those were brief laboratory tasks run online, testing acute effects rather than weeks of practice;[^2] a longer trial could find something different.

**Unknowns:** whether the effects come from the sound or from the slowed breathing chanting requires; whether any particular syllable, language or tradition matters, since no study compared them; most psychiatric diagnoses apart from PTSD.

**Safety is under-investigated.** Only four of the 51 studies reported adverse events at all, which the reviewers flag as a gap.[^1]

---

## Who should be careful

No meaningful risks. Chanting and humming are among the safest practices available, and no harms were reported across the 51 studies reviewed — though only four of them recorded adverse events at all, so that absence carries less weight than it appears to.[^1]

One practical note: humming is audible. It is quieter and less conspicuous than chanting, not silent.

---

## Traditional convergence

Very strong, and about as broad as convergence gets. Sustained vocal repetition appears in Hindu and Buddhist mantra, Christian psalmody and the Jesus Prayer, Islamic dhikr, Jewish nigunim, Sikh kirtan, and the ceremonial chant of indigenous traditions across every inhabited continent.[^4]

These traditions differ on almost everything else. They converged on repeating a sustained sound.

And when two of them were measured side by side — the Latin Ave Maria of the Catholic rosary and a yoga mantra, in 23 adults — both produced the same breathing rate of six per minute, with synchronised cardiovascular rhythms and raised baroreflex sensitivity.[^3] Two traditions, no contact, entirely different purposes, neither trying to regulate anything physiological, arriving at the same rate.
