# Music and deliberately chosen sound

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for acute stress, surgical pain and subjective sleep quality, **Preliminary** for everyday stress recovery in healthy adults and for binaural beats or specific tuning frequencies.

**Traditional convergence — Very strong** *(deliberate listening to music and sound used independently to regulate mood and attention across Greek, Islamic, Indian and East Asian traditions for centuries)*.

> **In short:** listening to chosen music reliably lowers acute anxiety and physical arousal in high-stress and medical settings,[^1] and cuts pain and painkiller need around surgery.[^2] For sleep, bedtime music produces a solid improvement in how well people *feel* they slept, while leaving objective sleep architecture largely unchanged.[^3] The main catch is that most strong trials come from hospitals and clinics: in healthy adults recovering from a laboratory stressor, pooled results show no significant advantage overall.[^4] And despite heavy marketing, neither binaural beats nor special tuning frequencies have established reliable, distinct effects beyond ordinary listening.[^8]

---

## What it is

Using recorded or live music, natural soundscapes, or deliberate attention to environmental sound to shift arousal, mood or focus. Unlike singing or chanting, the body is not producing the sound — the nervous system is responding to what it hears and how attention is placed on it.

Two distinct ingredients are often run together: **what is played** (music, water, birdsong, ambient sound) and **how you listen** (passive background audio versus giving the sound undivided attention). Most clinical trials test passive listening through speakers or headphones, rather than attentive listening as a contemplative practice.[^1]

---

## What it does for you

### It lowers physiological and psychological stress — especially in high-stress settings

Pooling **104 randomised controlled trials covering 9,617 participants**, music interventions reduced both physiological stress markers (**d = 0.380**, 95% CI [0.30, 0.47]) and psychological stress and anxiety (**d = 0.545**, 95% CI [0.43, 0.66]).[^1] Within the physiological measures, the largest drop appeared in **heart rate (d = 0.456)**, alongside smaller reductions in **stress hormone levels (d = 0.349)** and **blood pressure (d = 0.343)**.[^1]

Most of those studies (85% of the clinical subset) took place in medical or therapeutic settings rather than ordinary daily life.[^4] In that context, the effect is small-to-moderate and consistent.[^1]

### It reduces acute pain and anxiety around medical procedures

A systematic review of **73 randomised trials in adults undergoing surgery** found that listening to music reduced postoperative **pain (SMD = −0.77**, 95% CI [−0.99, −0.56]), **anxiety (SMD = −0.68**, 95% CI [−0.95, −0.41]), and **painkiller use (SMD = −0.37**, 95% CI [−0.54, −0.20]), while increasing patient satisfaction (**SMD = 1.09**).[^2]

The reductions appeared whether music was played before, during or after surgery — and even when patients were under general anaesthetic — though length of hospital stay did not change.[^2]

### It improves how well people feel they sleep

A Cochrane review of **13 randomised trials (1,007 adults with insomnia symptoms)** found moderate-certainty evidence that bedtime music improved subjective sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index by **2.79 points** (95% CI [−3.86, −1.72]) compared with no treatment or usual care — roughly a one-standard-deviation improvement.[^3]

However, two trials measuring overall insomnia severity showed no clear difference, and three trials measuring sleep objectively did not find improvements in time to fall asleep, total sleep time or sleep interruptions.[^3]

### Natural sounds reduce stress and lift mood

A synthesis of **36 studies** (with **18 meta-analysed**) found that listening to natural soundscapes reduced stress and annoyance (**g = −0.60**, 95% CI [−0.97, −0.23]) and improved positive affect and health outcomes (**g = 1.63**, 95% CI [0.09, 3.16]).[^5] Water sounds produced the largest average gains in mood and health measures, while birdsong did the most to reduce stress and annoyance.[^5]

### Broad music engagement produces modest gains in mental quality of life

Across **26 trials (779 participants)** pooling music listening (10 studies), music therapy (7 studies) and singing (8 to 9 studies), adding music improved mental health quality-of-life scores on the SF-36 by **2.95 points** (95% CI [1.39, 4.51]) and physical scores by **1.09 points** (95% CI [0.15, 2.03]).[^6] No single genre or format proved superior, and individual responses varied widely.[^6]

---

## How much you need to do

- **25 to 60 minutes before bed** (averaging about 36 minutes a night) for three days to three months was the range across the insomnia trials.[^3]
- **Short acute sessions** (often 15 to 45 minutes around a stressor or medical procedure) are what the surgical and stress trials tested.[^1][^2]
- **No optimal dose or genre is established.** Across quality-of-life trials, effect sizes did not differ reliably by intervention type, genre or duration.[^6]

**So: 20 to 40 minutes of unhurried listening — before sleep or during recovery from stress — matches what the trials tested.** Choose sound or music you genuinely find settling rather than forcing a prescribed genre.

## Where the evidence stops

**Everyday stress recovery in healthy adults is inconclusive.** When a meta-analysis isolated **14 randomised laboratory studies (706 healthy adults)** who listened to music immediately after an acute stressor, the cumulative effect on stress recovery was **not statistically significant (g = 0.15**, 95% CI [−0.21, 0.52], *p* = .374).[^4] Outcomes varied sharply by who chose the music, tempo and genre, leaving no firm conclusion for healthy people outside clinical distress.[^4]

**Objective sleep architecture does not reliably change.** In a crossover pilot trial measuring overnight polysomnography in **20 healthy adults**, **30 minutes** of music listening before bed produced no clinically meaningful changes in sleep stages and did not improve subjective sleep quality.[^7] That aligns with the Cochrane finding that subjective sleep ratings improve far more reliably than objective sleep timing.[^3]

**Binaural beats do not reliably entrain brainwaves.** A systematic review of **14 EEG studies** testing whether binaural beats synchronise cortical oscillations to a target frequency found **eight contradictory studies**, five supportive studies and one mixed result, with wide methodological inconsistency.[^8] Buying tracks labelled "theta for deep meditation" or "delta for sleep" goes well beyond what the brain data support.

**Specific tuning frequencies lack firm clinical backing.** While small pilot trials have tested music tuned to **432 Hz** against standard **440 Hz** in settings like emergency nursing,[^9] the evidence base is tiny and exploratory — there is no meta-analytic basis for treating specific Hertz numbers as distinct medical or psychological interventions.

**Unknowns:** whether attentive, silent-room *Conscious Listening* (treating sound itself as the object of meditation rather than background comfort) produces different effects from passive music listening; why individual responses vary so widely across the same tracks;[^6] and how much of the bedtime benefit comes from masking intrusive thoughts or household noise rather than acoustics alone.[^3]

**And one thing to actively disbelieve:** that a specific frequency number, "brainwave-entrainment" track or prescribed genre carries guaranteed biological effects regardless of whether you actually like listening to it. Individual preference and context drive much of the response.[^4][^6]

---

## Who should be careful

No adverse events were reported across the 13 insomnia trials in the Cochrane review,[^3] making music and natural sound listening very low risk overall. One documented psychological caution does apply.

**Depression and rumination.** In a study of **175 adults**, listening to self-selected sad music significantly *increased* depressed mood in listeners high in ruminative tendencies — even though people prone to depression reported liking and seeking out sad music.[^10] If putting on melancholic tracks leaves you looping on what is wrong rather than comforted, switch to neutral soundscapes, instrumental music or silence.

*The two practical cautions below are not derived from the clinical trials cited here, and are marked as such.*

**Listening volume.** Prolonged high-volume listening through headphones or earbuds damages hearing over time. Keep volume moderate, especially if using audio nightly for sleep.

**Situational awareness.** Do not use headphones or deeply absorbed listening practices when driving, cycling or walking in traffic where hearing your surroundings is a safety requirement.

## Traditional convergence

Very strong. Traditions that never met independently observed that deliberate listening alters emotional state and attention — and independently cautioned that the effect depends on the listener as much as the sound.

In ancient Greece, Aristotle analysed in *Politics* how different musical modes and rhythms shift the listener's emotional disposition — some settling the mind into a moderate temper, others exciting it — and treated music as a means for both rest and emotional release (*katharsis*).[^11] In Islamic thought, Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī devoted Book 18 of the *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* to spiritual listening (*samāʿ*), arguing that sound does not put into the listener what is not already there, but stirs whatever state is already present — making context and intention decisive.[^11] And in East Asian Mahāyāna Buddhism, the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* places Avalokiteśvara's ear-organ practice at the centre of contemplative training: rather than chasing external sounds with likes and dislikes, the practitioner turns attention directly onto the act of hearing itself until agitation settles.[^11]

You can test the practical core directly: sit for ten minutes with a single piece of music or natural sound without checking a phone or doing another task, and notice whether your attention settles, wanders or intensifies the mood you brought in.
