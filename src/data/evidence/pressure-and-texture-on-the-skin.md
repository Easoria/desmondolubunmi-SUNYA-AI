# Pressure and texture on the skin

*Reviewed November 2026*

**Research strength — Moderate** for steady pressure improving sleep quality, **Preliminary** for pressure on anxiety, stress and pain, **Mechanistic only** for fabric and material, **None found** for cut and shape.

**Traditional convergence — Weak** *(the traditions regulate clothing heavily, but on different grounds — see below)*.

> **In short:** something is in contact with your skin at every moment of your life, including the eight hours you are asleep, and it turns out to be doing more than nothing. In the best trial available, 102 adults with insomnia were given either a 5.5 kg weighted blanket or an identical-looking 3.5 kg one with the weighting left out; the heavier blanket improved sleep quality significantly more, along with daytime sleepiness, stress, anxiety, fatigue and bodily pain.[^1] That is an unusually honest comparison — the control group got the same object minus the one ingredient being tested — and the effect survived it, which most things in this evidence base do not. On the material side, the body demonstrably registers the difference between fabrics: EEG recordings distinguish cotton from nylon from a polyester-wool blend, and those neural differences track both what people report feeling and the fabrics' measured physical properties.[^2] What nobody has tested is whether choosing one fabric over another changes how you feel over a day or a week.

---

## What it is

Two things that happen to be the same mechanism from the body's point of view.

**Pressure** — how much steady weight or compression is resting on the skin. A weighted blanket, a firm waistband, a close-fitting garment, bedding.

**Texture** — what the surface in contact with you actually feels like. The fibre, the weave, the roughness, whether it holds moisture against the skin.

Both reach the nervous system through the same route: the skin is the body's largest sensory organ, reporting continuously whether you attend to it or not. The question this page asks is whether any of that reporting changes how you feel — and the answer differs sharply depending on which half you are asking about.

## What it does for you

### Steady pressure improves sleep quality — and it beat a real control

This is the strongest thing on the page, and it is worth understanding why.

A trial across three hospitals randomised **102 adults with insomnia** to sleep under either a 5.5 kg weighted blanket or a 3.5 kg blanket of **identical shape with the glass beads left out**, for one month.[^1] That second group matters enormously. Most wellness research compares a practice against doing nothing, which measures the practice plus the expectation. Here the control group got the same blanket, looking the same, weighing something — so most of what is left is the weight itself.

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Sleep quality (PSQI) | −4.1 vs −2.0, **p = 0.006** |
| Daytime sleepiness (ESS) | **p = 0.017** |
| Stress, anxiety and fatigue | all **p < 0.05** |
| Bodily pain (SF-36) | **p = 0.010** |

Retention was **93%**,[^1] which tells you something on its own: people kept using it. Nothing is asked of the person here — no practice, no discipline, no time set aside. It works while you are unconscious.

Two things did **not** move, and the page says so: the insomnia-specific severity index (p = 0.233) and depression (p = 0.126).[^1] Objective awakenings measured by actigraphy did not change either.[^1] So the fair reading is that sleep gets better in how it is experienced, rather than insomnia being treated.

### Pressure shortens the time it takes to fall asleep in some groups

In children with ADHD, weighted blankets reduced sleep-onset latency significantly, across two separate studies.[^3] A trial in healthy adults found weighted blankets raised pre-sleep salivary melatonin.[^3] That is a plausible physiological route to the sleep finding above, though it has not been established as the explanation.

### Brief use reduces anxiety in people who are frightened

Patients undergoing chemotherapy reported significantly lower anxiety after **30 minutes** under a weighted blanket, and a separate study in inpatient mental health settings found a significant anxiety reduction.[^3] In people with dementia, ten minutes of use reduced distressed vocalisations.[^3]

These are small studies without strong controls, so this sits at **Preliminary**. But the pattern — acute comfort within minutes, in people under acute stress — is consistent across several unconnected settings.

### The body genuinely registers what fabric is against it

Twelve adults had EEG recorded while cotton, nylon and a polyester-wool blend were applied to the skin.[^2] Theta and gamma power differed significantly between fabrics (p < 0.05), and those neural differences correlated both with what participants said they felt and with the fabrics' physical properties as measured instrumentally (p < 0.05).[^2]

So this is not imagination. The difference between a soft fabric and a scratchy one is a measurable neural event, and the brain's response lines up with the physics of the cloth.

**What has not been shown is that it matters to your wellbeing.** No trial has given people one fabric rather than another and measured mood, stress or sleep. This is the clearest example in the whole evidence base of a mechanism that is well established at the level of physiology and entirely unstudied at the level of outcome — which is what the **Mechanistic only** grade exists to mark.

## How much you need to do

- **The sleep result came from a 5.5 kg blanket used nightly for one month.**[^1] That is the one dose with good outcome evidence behind it.
- **The acute anxiety findings came from 10 to 30 minutes.**[^3]
- **The pain trial used 6.8 kg for seven nights.**[^4]

**And one thing to disbelieve:** the widely repeated rule that a weighted blanket should be **10% of your body weight** has no trial behind it. No study has compared weights to find an optimum. The trials above used 5.5 kg and 6.8 kg on ordinary adult populations and did not test alternatives.

For fabric, there is no dose, because there is no outcome evidence to take a dose from.

## Where the evidence stops

**The pain result is mostly negative, and should not be quoted as positive.** A double-blind randomised trial of 118 adults with chronic pain found **no significant effect on its primary outcome** — pain intensity — in either the brief trial or after seven nights.[^4] A secondary composite measure did improve significantly with the heavier blanket (−1.4 vs −0.7, p = 0.04),[^4] and the effect was larger in people with higher trait anxiety (p = 0.02),[^4] but a null primary outcome with a positive secondary is a weak signal, not a finding. Neither anxiety nor sleep quality showed the expected interaction effect, and neither mediated the pain result.[^4]

**The sleep literature is small and mostly uncontrolled.** The review covering this area describes itself as narrative, reports no pooled effect sizes, and notes that most included studies had fewer than 100 participants.[^3] One of the better-controlled studies — 67 children with autism, randomised, placebo-controlled, crossover — found that subjective sleep *feeling* improved significantly while **total sleep time, sleep-onset latency and sleep efficiency did not.**[^3]

That pattern repeats across this mechanism: what improves is how sleep and the body are experienced, more consistently than what instruments record. Worth knowing, and not a reason to dismiss it — how you experience your night is the thing you actually live.

**Nothing links fabric choice to any health or mood outcome.** Searched under clothing comfort, sensorial comfort, next-to-skin textiles, tactile comfort and wear comfort; the literature is large, technically sophisticated and concerned almost entirely with rating comfort and characterising materials. The outcome studies do not exist.

**Nothing at all was found on cut and shape** — whether the silhouette or structure of a garment affects how a person feels, independent of pressure and texture. Note that research on *enclothed cognition* is deliberately excluded here: that studies what clothes mean symbolically, which is a different mechanism and does not belong on this page.

## Who should be careful

**Weighted blankets are not safe for everyone, and this is one of the few places in the library where weight itself is the hazard.**

Children under three years old, or under about 23 kg, should not use them at all, because of suffocation and entrapment risk; a child's face must never be covered.[^3] Heavier blankets have caused pain, anxiety or panic in some children.[^3]

Older adults must be able to remove the blanket themselves without help.[^3] Anyone frail, with limited mobility or with severe dementia should use the lightest option and ask a doctor first.[^3] The review also advises caution or avoidance with coronary heart disease, stroke history, respiratory problems, cancer and severe cognitive impairment.[^3]

In the insomnia trial, no severe adverse events occurred, though three participants reported feeling cold, and one person withdrew for that reason.[^1]

**Tight clothing has a documented harm pathway.** Sustained pressure at the hip can compress the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and produce numbness, burning and tingling across the outer thigh — a condition called meralgia paresthetica. It was described in truck drivers wearing tight jeans, where the pressure at the inguinal crease combined with long hours of hip flexion; those who switched to loose trousers reported notable improvement.[^5] Case reports have continued since. This is the clearest evidence on this page that clothing tightness does something, and it happens to be evidence of harm rather than benefit.

## Traditional convergence

**Weak, and the reason is interesting enough to state plainly rather than paper over.**

The contemplative traditions regulate clothing extensively. Buddhist monastic codes specify the number of robes, their permitted materials and how they are to be obtained and repaired; Hindu, Christian and Sufi monastic orders all prescribe dress in detail.[^6]

But they do so on grounds of **renunciation, simplicity, modesty and the marking of a community** — not on the grounds that a particular fabric against the skin changes the nervous system. There is no widespread traditional claim here for the research to converge with.

This is a case where the honest answer is that the traditions and the research are talking about different things. It is included in this evidence base because the grading has two axes, and a mechanism can earn a place on one of them alone.
